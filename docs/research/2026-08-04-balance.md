# Is the both-poles balance premise supportable?

**Question.** SCIENCE.md asserts that three OCEAN dimensions require *balance* in
a team, meaning representation at **both poles**: Extraversion (Presence),
Agreeableness (Bond), Openness (Vision); and that Conscientiousness and
Neuroticism are directional instead. An audit established that the two cited
sources do not support this. This report asks whether the wider literature does.

**Short answer.** The premise is one third arguable and two thirds wrong, and it
has the wrong theoretical shape even where it is arguable.

- **Extraversion.** The only one with a case. Three meta-analyses put the
  variance-performance point estimate at a small *positive* value (Bell 2007
  r̄ = +.03; Prewett et al. 2009 r̄ = +.06; Peeters et al. 2006 ρ = +0.06), and it
  has never reached significance in any of them. A published theoretical
  rationale exists and is specific to Extraversion: the complementary-fit
  tradition, and the interpersonal circumplex rule that dominance is
  complementary while warmth is reciprocal. Two recent team-level studies run
  against it. Verdict: **unresolved, weakly favourable prior, actively disputed.
  Narrow, do not delete.**
- **Agreeableness.** Contradicted consistently by every meta-analysis, and by the
  same theoretical tradition Extraversion's case rests on, which says warmth is
  *reciprocal* rather than complementary. Verdict: **replace.**
- **Openness.** No signal in either direction; the only significant variance
  estimates anywhere are negative (field and professional teams). Verdict:
  **replace.**
- **Conscientiousness.** Cèrcol is right and can say it more strongly:
  directional up *and* dispersion-averse.
- **Neuroticism.** Cèrcol is currently overclaiming. There is no reliable
  mean-level effect at team level in either direction.

The honest structure is therefore not "three balance dimensions and two
directional ones". It is: **one dimension with a genuine, contested
complementarity case (E); one with a genuine similarity case (A); one with no
case either way (O); one clearly directional and dispersion-averse (C); one with
no team-level evidence at all (N).**

A separate finding, in section 8: the AB5C says nothing about teams, and no
published team-role taxonomy is derived from a personality circumplex. Using the
AB5C to define team roles is Cèrcol's own extension, and SCIENCE.md currently
implies otherwise. The exception is the four Presence × Bond roles, which are the
four quadrants of the interpersonal circumplex, a published and replicated
structure specifically about interpersonal behaviour (McCrae & Costa 1989). That
is real cover, and Cèrcol is not currently claiming it.

Verdicts per dimension are in section 7, replacement text in section 9.
Everything below was read at the source stated; blocked items are declared in
section 10.

## 1. The decisive source: Peeters et al. (2006)

This is the paper that settles the question, and it is the one paper SCIENCE.md
does not cite. It is a meta-analysis that tested exactly the distinction Cèrcol
needs: **elevation** (team mean) versus **variability** (team variance/SD), for
each Big Five factor, against team performance.

> Peeters, M. A. G., Van Tuijl, H. F. J. M., Rutte, C. G., & Reymen, I. M. M. J.
> (2006). Personality and team performance: a meta-analysis. *European Journal
> of Personality*, 20(5), 377–396. https://doi.org/10.1002/per.588

Route: full published PDF, open in the University of Twente repository at
`https://ris.utwente.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/6619120/personality_and_team_performance.pdf`,
text extracted locally with `pdftotext -layout`. DOI verified via the Crossref
API.

Abstract, verbatim (p. 377):

> "Using a meta-analytical procedure, the relationship between team composition
> in terms of the Big-Five personality traits (trait elevation and variability)
> and team performance were researched. The number of teams upon which analyses
> were performed ranged from 106 to 527. For the total sample, significant
> effects were found for elevation in agreeableness (ρ = 0.24) and
> conscientiousness (ρ = 0.20), and for variability in agreeableness (ρ = −0.12)
> and conscientiousness (ρ = −0.24). Moderation by type of team was tested for
> professional teams versus student teams."

### Conclusions, verbatim

Summary for A and C (p. 389):

> "So, the higher the average level of agreeableness and conscientiousness
> within teams, and the more similar team members are with respect to
> agreeableness and conscientiousness, the better their team performs."

Extraversion (p. 392):

> "Neither elevation nor variability in extraversion influences team
> performance. For elevation this is in line with what we expected. For
> variability we expected a positive relationship with team performance, but
> although results were in the predicted direction (especially in the
> professional team sample), they were not significant. Based on our results we
> can only conclude that both elevation and variability in extraversion are not
> related to team performance."

Agreeableness (p. 392):

> "In general, we can conclude that teams whose members score both highly
> (except for student teams) and similarly on agreeableness are the teams that
> perform best."

Emotional stability (p. 393):

> "Contrary to expectations, elevation in emotional stability is not positively
> related to team performance. [...] Given the small magnitude of effects at the
> general level and the opposing effects at the sub sample level, we restrain
> ourselves from drawing general conclusions for this trait."

Openness (p. 393):

> "Elevation in openness to experience is not positively related to team
> performance, like we expected it to be. [...] we feel it is unjustifiable to
> draw general conclusions regarding openness to experience on the basis of the
> present results."

### Why this is the sharpest possible test of the Cèrcol premise

Peeters is not a hostile source. They *pre-registered Cèrcol's hypothesis* for
Extraversion. Their H1b (p. 381) reads:

> "H1b Variability in extraversion is positively related to team performance."

and their reasoning is almost word for word Cèrcol's Presence bullet (p. 380–381):

> "researchers also express caution over the inclusion of too many extraverts in
> a team, since this may harm the team's effectiveness. [...] This focus on
> pleasurable social interaction is expected to distract their attention from
> task completion [...] The emphasis of researchers on either positive or
> negative effects of the elevation of extraversion at the same time forms the
> basis for the expectation of a positive effect of variability in
> extraversion."

They also pre-registered the *opposite* of Cèrcol's premise for Agreeableness
(H2b: "Variability in agreeableness is negatively related to team performance")
and Openness (H5b: "Variability in openness to experience is not related to team
performance"), and both were confirmed.

So the both-poles idea for Extraversion is a hypothesis the field formulated,
tested, and did not confirm. And the both-poles idea for Agreeableness is one the
field predicted would fail, tested, and confirmed the failure of.

Footnote 1 (p. 380) also gives the within-team correlations between elevation and
variability, which will matter for Cèrcol's own future analysis: extraversion
−0.08, agreeableness −0.36, conscientiousness −0.22, emotional stability 0.01,
openness −0.26. Elevation and variability are not independent.

## 2. Convergence across meta-analyses

Combining Peeters with the three other meta-analyses that coded a variance
operationalisation gives an unusually clean picture. Values are as published:
Peeters and Bell report ρ (corrected), Prewett and Carter report r̄
(sample-weighted observed), van Dijk reports r.

**Team mean / elevation:**

| Factor | Bell 2007 ρ | Peeters 2006 ρ | Carter 2019 r |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extraversion | .10 | .04 | .10 |
| Agreeableness | .12 | **.24** | .14 |
| Conscientiousness | .14 | **.20** | .08 |
| Emotional stability | .13 | .04 | .13 |
| Openness | .11 | .03 | .13 |

**Team variance / diversity:**

| Factor | Bell 2007 | Prewett 2009 r̄ | Peeters 2006 ρ | Carter 2019 r | van Dijk 2012 r |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Extraversion** | **+.03** (r̄), ns | **+.06** | **+0.06**, ns | ns | n/a |
| Agreeableness | −.04 (ρ), −.03 (r̄) | −.07 | **−0.12** | −.14 | −.03 |
| Conscientiousness | −.03 | −.06 | **−0.24** | −.12 | −.09 |
| Emotional stability | ns | n/a | 0.02, ns | n/a | n/a |
| Openness | −.02, negative in field | not coded | −0.01 ns, **−0.11** in professional teams | n/a | n/a |

Sources for the Bell, Prewett and Carter cells:

- Bell's own ρ values as reproduced inline, each attributed to "Bell, 2007", in
  Driskell, T., Funke, G., Tolston, M., Capiola, A., & Driskell, J. (2024).
  Composition considerations for fluid teams: a review. *Frontiers in
  Psychology*, 15, 1302022. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1302022 (open
  access, read directly).
- Bell's and Prewett's r̄ cell values as tabulated side by side in Simon, K. M.
  (2013), *Team intelligence and team personality predicting team performance*,
  PhD dissertation, University of Minnesota (open, UMN Digital Conservancy),
  pp. 31, 47, 60, 70, 115.
- Bell's field-setting values and overall ρ with SDρ in Qu, J. (2024),
  *Deep-level team composition predicting team processes and effectiveness: a
  meta-analysis*, PhD dissertation, University of Minnesota (open, UMN Digital
  Conservancy), pp. 7, 25, 27, 30, and Table 1 p. 164.
- Carter, K. M., Mead, B. A., Stewart, G. L., Nielsen, J. D., & Solimeo, S. L.
  (2019). Reviewing work team design characteristics across industries:
  combining meta-analysis and comprehensive synthesis. *Small Group Research*,
  50(1), 138–188. https://doi.org/10.1177/1046496418797431, values via Driskell
  et al. (2024).
- van Dijk, H., van Engen, M. L., & van Knippenberg, D. (2012). Defying
  conventional wisdom: a meta-analytical examination of the differences between
  demographic and job-related diversity relationships with performance.
  *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 119(1), 38–53.
  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2012.06.003, values via Driskell et al. (2024).

**What this table says.**

1. Extraversion is the *only* factor whose variance estimate is positive, and it
   is positive in all three meta-analyses that estimated it. It is also never
   significant in any of them. Qu (2024, p. 30) summarises Bell, Prewett and
   Simon as all finding "zero or close-to-zero" relationships for extraversion
   heterogeneity.
2. Agreeableness variance is negative in five out of five estimates.
3. Openness variance is null overall and negative wherever it is significant,
   which is field settings (Bell) and professional teams (Peeters). Driskell et
   al. (2024, p. 8) note that "both Bell and Peeters et al. showed that diversity
   negatively impacted performance for teams in field settings" for openness.
4. Bell's field-setting agreeableness effects are the strongest composition
   results in the literature and they are *elevation and minimum* effects:
   minimum agreeableness ρ = .37 and mean agreeableness ρ = .34 in field settings
   (Qu 2024, pp. 7 and 25).

Point 4 matters for a specific SCIENCE.md claim. Bell's headline field result is
about the *minimum* Agreeableness in a team, which is the "one disagreeable
person spoils it" story. That is the exact opposite of a both-poles requirement:
it says the low pole is the thing to avoid.

### Correction to the current SCIENCE.md text on Bell and Extraversion

SCIENCE.md line 40 says "Neither study examined Extraversion", and line 80 says
"Extraversion is in neither paper's findings." Both are too strong for Bell.
Bell coded all five factors under all four operationalisations, and her field
results do include Extraversion. Two independent sources say so:

- Driskell et al. (2024, p. 8): Bell's extraversion findings show the
  relationship to be significant only for teams in field settings.
- Mathieu, J. E., Gallagher, P. T., Domingo, M. A., & Klock, E. A. (2019).
  Embracing complexity: reviewing the past decade of team effectiveness
  research. *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational
  Behavior*, 6, 17–46, p. 25: Bell (2007) demonstrated that in the field a
  team's average conscientiousness, agreeableness and extraversion positively
  predicted performance.

The accurate statement is: Bell does report Extraversion; the mean-level effect
is small (ρ = .10 overall) and significant only in field settings; and Bell's
Extraversion **heterogeneity** estimate is +.03 and non-significant. So Bell
supports neither the current "not in the findings" wording nor a both-poles
claim.

### Prewett et al. (2009) framed the exact distinction Cèrcol needs

> Prewett, M. S., Walvoord, A. A. G., Stilson, F. R. B., Rossi, M. E., &
> Brannick, M. T. (2009). The team personality–team performance relationship
> revisited: the impact of criterion choice, pattern of workflow, and method of
> aggregation. *Human Performance*, 22(4), 273–296.
> https://doi.org/10.1080/08959280903120253

Route: complete abstract read on the USF Digital Commons metadata record
(`digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/2317/`); cell values from Simon (2013) and
Qu (2024) as above. The article itself is Taylor & Francis-gated.

Their framing: trait theory predicts that maximising the team level is *harmful*
for **Extraversion** (complementary fit) but *beneficial* for **Conscientiousness,
Agreeableness and Emotional Stability** (supplementary fit). Their findings gave
partial support to both, with C and A showing supplementary fit primarily on team
behaviours, and **mixed** evidence that Extraversion shows complementary fit.

Two things follow. First, the complementary/supplementary distinction Cèrcol
needs is a real, published construct in exactly this literature. Second, the
literature applies it to **Extraversion alone**, and applies the *opposite*
(supplementary, i.e. similarity and elevation) to Agreeableness. Cèrcol currently
treats E and A identically. Note also that Prewett et al. **omitted Openness
entirely**, on theoretical grounds.

Qu (2024, p. 8) records Prewett's sign pattern: positive for extraversion
heterogeneity, negative for agreeableness heterogeneity, no effect for
conscientiousness heterogeneity; and that even where 95% CIs excluded zero, the
heterogeneity effects were **weaker than the mean effects**.

### The most recent meta-analysis

> Han, A., Krieger, F., Kim, S., Nixon, N., & Greiff, S. (2024). Revisiting the
> relationship between team members' personality and their team's performance: a
> meta-analysis. *Journal of Research in Personality*, 112, 104526.
> https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrp.2024.104526

Abstract, verbatim (route: Yonsei University Pure repository record):

> "The relationships between team members' personality traits and their team's
> performance have been studied for decades, yet the relationships remain
> unclear. [...] We focused on task performance and the means and standard
> deviations of team members' Big Five personality traits. The results revealed
> that several team personality traits were weakly related to team performance
> and key moderators influenced the relationships."

Per-trait values are behind Elsevier's paywall and were not obtained. The
publisher highlights note that the standard deviation of neuroticism shows the
highest correlation, which is not a result that helps any of Cèrcol's three
balance dimensions.

## 3. Barry & Stewart (1997), the classic curvilinear extraversion study

> Barry, B., & Stewart, G. L. (1997). Composition, process, and performance in
> self-managed groups: the role of personality. *Journal of Applied Psychology*,
> 82(1), 62–78. https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.82.1.62

Route: abstract obtained verbatim from PubMed via the NCBI E-utilities API
(PMID 9119798). **The full text could not be obtained.** See section 9.

Abstract, verbatim:

> "Using the five factor model with an emphasis on extraversion and
> conscientiousness, the authors investigated how personality is related to
> small group processes and outcomes. Graduate students (N = 289) assigned to
> 4- and 5-person teams in 61 groups engaged in a series of creative
> problem-solving tasks over a period of several weeks. Extraversion was
> associated with group processes and outcomes at both individual and group
> levels of analysis. At the individual level, extraverts were perceived by
> others as having greater effect than introverts on group outcomes. Covariance
> structure modeling suggested that extraverts induce these perceptions through
> the provision of both socioemotional and task-related inputs. At the group
> level, the proportion of relatively extraverted members was related
> curvilinearly to task focus and group performance. Contrary to expectations,
> Conscientiousness was unrelated to processes and outcomes at either the
> individual or group level."

Sample corroborated independently from Peeters Table 1 (p. 387): student teams,
n = 61 groups, mean size 4.7, tenure two semesters, three tasks of one week each,
instructor ratings blind to predictor scores.

**What it does and does not support.**

1. It is a **curvilinear elevation** finding, not a variance finding. The
   predictor is the *proportion of relatively extraverted members*. Peeters
   classes it as an elevation operationalisation and flags it as a
   methodological caveat (p. 394): "For elevation, Barry and Stewart (1997) used
   proportions of high scoring team members, whereas the other studies used
   means or sums."
2. An inverted U on the proportion of extraverts says *avoid extreme
   compositions*. That is materially weaker than "a team needs representation at
   both poles". A team uniformly average on Extraversion satisfies the inverted U
   perfectly and Cèrcol's premise not at all. Cèrcol's role centroids sit at
   z = ±1.0, which is exactly the composition an inverted U penalises.
3. Bell, Brown, Colaneri & Outland (2018, accepted manuscript pp. 13–14)
   describe the same result as the proportion of high-extraversion members being
   **negatively** related to task focus, curvilinearly, with the decrement
   *levelling off* at higher extraversion, and explain it as extraverts
   communicating more but not necessarily about taskwork. On that reading the
   curve is a decelerating decline rather than a hump with an interior optimum,
   which would remove the interior-optimum interpretation entirely.
4. It is one study, 61 student groups, one creative-problem-solving paradigm, and
   its conscientiousness prediction failed outright.

**Caution about the "20–40%" figure.** Multiple secondary summaries state that
performance peaked when 20–40% of the team scored in the top third on
extraversion. That number is not in the abstract and I could not verify it
against the primary source. Given point 3 above, two different secondary readings
of the same result are in circulation. **Do not put the figure in SCIENCE.md
without a library copy of pp. 62–78.**

## 4. What happened to the Extraversion-variance hypothesis after Peeters

This matters most, because Extraversion is the only balance dimension with a live
research programme, and the programme has run.

### 4.1 The one strongly supportive study: Humphrey et al. (2007)

> Humphrey, S. E., Hollenbeck, J. R., Meyer, C. J., & Ilgen, D. R. (2007). Trait
> configurations in self-managed teams: a conceptual examination of the use of
> seeding for maximizing and minimizing trait variance in teams. *Journal of
> Applied Psychology*, 92(3), 885–892.
> https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.3.885

Route: **abstract not obtained verbatim** (see section 9). The finding as
reported by later papers, including den Hartog et al. (2020) which was read in
full: a longitudinal study of MBA teams found that the combination of
**minimising conscientiousness variance and maximising extraversion variance**
produced the highest short-term and long-term performance, and that the two
variances interact.

This is the single best pro-premise finding in the literature for Extraversion,
and it is exactly the asymmetry Cèrcol wants: E benefits from spread, C does not.
**But treat it as unverified at source, and see 4.2.**

### 4.2 The same authors did not reproduce it

den Hartog et al. (2020, accepted manuscript p. 8):

> "A larger study with self-managed teams (Humphrey, Hollenbeck, Meyer, & Ilgen,
> 2011) did not find effects of heterogeneity in extraversion on performance or
> team effectiveness."

That study is Humphrey, S. E., Hollenbeck, J. R., Meyer, C. J., & Ilgen, D. R.
(2011). Personality configurations in self-managed teams: a natural experiment on
the effects of maximizing and minimizing variance in traits. *Journal of Applied
Social Psychology*, 41(7), 1701–1732.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1559-1816.2011.00778.x (DOI verified via Crossref; full
text Wiley-gated).

So the strongest pro-premise result is a single 2007 study that its own authors
did not reproduce in a larger natural experiment four years later.

### 4.3 The most recent team-level test found the opposite

> den Hartog, S. C., Runge, J. M., Reindl, G., & Lang, J. W. B. (2020). Linking
> personality trait variance in self-managed teams to team innovation. *Small
> Group Research*, 51(2), 265–295. https://doi.org/10.1177/1046496419865325

Route: green open-access accepted manuscript at Ghent University,
`https://biblio.ugent.be/publication/8632707/file/8737611.pdf` (full PDF read).

Abstract, verbatim:

> "Researchers have suggested that some personality traits are associated with
> better team functioning when team members are homogeneous, whereas other
> personality traits improve team functioning when team members are
> heterogeneous. This article extends these ideas to team innovation and
> examines (a) how team variance in extraversion, agreeableness, openness, and
> conscientiousness relates to innovation in teams; and (b) how these
> relationships dynamically evolve over time. Our study included 704 surveys
> completed by 243 team members in 32 teams, at three time points. Results
> revealed that teams with less variance in extraversion showed higher levels of
> team innovation. For agreeableness and openness, we did not find main effects
> of team heterogeneity on team innovation. For teams with low heterogeneity in
> agreeableness, however, team innovation decreased over time. Team variance in
> conscientiousness was negatively associated with team innovation. Our findings
> provide support that team personality plays a role for innovation."

Multilevel results (accepted manuscript pp. 21–23):

- Extraversion variance → innovation intercept: **γ = −0.22, t(32.00) = −4.18,
  p < .001**.
- Extraversion variance → innovation slope: **γ = −0.11, t(65.70) = −3.28,
  p = .002**. Simple slopes: innovation fell over time for teams 1 SD above the
  mean in extraversion variance, t(63.65) = −4.50, p < .0001; no change for teams
  1 SD below, t(63.65) = 0.49, p = 0.62.
- Conscientiousness variance → intercept: **γ = −0.12, t(32.00) = −2.17,
  p = .037**.
- Agreeableness variance → intercept: not significant.
- Openness variance → intercept: not significant.
- Agreeableness variance → innovation slope: **γ = 0.07, t(62.70) = 2.15,
  p = .035**; innovation fell over time only for teams 1 SD *below* the mean in
  agreeableness variance, t(63.65) = −3.75, p < .001.

That last result is the only finding anywhere in this review that points toward a
both-poles benefit, and it is for **Agreeableness**, not Extraversion; it is an
over-time slope against a null main effect; and it is one study of 32 teams. Not
enough to build a dimension on, but honest to record.

Their theoretical section (pp. 8–9) is also worth quoting because it states the
Cèrcol argument and then argues against it:

> "Based on the idea that extraversion includes dominance, researchers have
> suggested that heterogeneity in extraversion in self-managed teams may be
> beneficial for team functioning because a team with dominant and less dominant
> members may result in better task delegation and working through tasks
> efficiently (Humphrey et al., 2007; Mohammed & Angell, 2003). [...] the
> tendency of the extraverted team members to talk more and dominate the
> conversation in the group (Humphrey et al., 2007) likely has negative
> consequences for team innovation. [...] introverted team members may stop
> talking altogether. As a result, opinions and perspectives of less extraverted
> team members may not be fully heard".

This is a direct, published rebuttal of Cèrcol's Presence bullet ("initiative and
listening must coexist"), and it was tested and supported.

### 4.4 A direct group-formation experiment on extraversion distribution

> Müller, A. M., Röpke, R., Konert, J., & Bellhäuser, H. (2024). Investigating
> group formation: an experiment on the distribution of extraversion in
> educational settings. *Acta Psychologica*, 242, 104111.
> https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2023.104111

Route: Europe PMC REST API, core result type. (The DOI resolves to a gold-OA
Elsevier article; Elsevier blocks non-browser clients, Europe PMC does not.)

Abstract, verbatim:

> "Group formation plays a crucial role in enhancing collaborative learning
> experiences. This study investigates the impact of extraversion as a criterion
> for group formation on collaborative learning outcomes. A total of 180
> students participated in the experiment and were assigned to groups that were
> homogeneously or heterogeneously distributed in terms of extraversion. The
> groups met weekly and worked on group assignments throughout the semesters.
> The first hypothesis posed the outcomes to be explainable at the group-level.
> Surprisingly, the results show that groups with a homogeneous distribution of
> extraversion reported higher levels of group work satisfaction than those with
> a heterogeneous distribution, in contrast to the second hypothesis and the
> group hierarchy theory."

A randomised composition experiment built on exactly Cèrcol's hypothesis,
motivated by group hierarchy theory, which came out the other way. A follow-up by
the same group exists (Müller, A., Goeddeke, A., Kneip, P., Konert, J., Röpke, R.,
& Bellhäuser, H. (2024). Experiment on extraversion distribution in groups
through a group formation algorithm. *Computers and Education Open*, 6, 100181.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeo.2024.100181, gold OA, DOI verified via Crossref)
but its abstract could not be retrieved (section 9).

### 4.5 Dyad-level evidence also favours similarity on Extraversion

> Cuperman, R., & Ickes, W. (2009). Big Five predictors of behavior and
> perceptions in initial dyadic interactions: personality similarity helps
> extraverts and introverts, but hurts "disagreeables". *Journal of Personality
> and Social Psychology*, 97(4), 667–684. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015741

Route: verbatim PubMed abstract via NCBI E-utilities (PMID 19785485).

> "The authors used the unstructured dyadic interaction paradigm to examine the
> effects of gender and the Big Five personality traits on dyad members'
> behaviors and perceptions in 87 initial, unstructured interactions. Most of
> the significant Big Five effects (84%) were associated with the traits of
> Extraversion and Agreeableness. [...] Personality similarity resulted in
> relatively good initial interactions for dyads composed of 2 extraverts or 2
> introverts, when compared with dissimilar (extravert–introvert) pairs.
> However, personality similarity resulted in uniquely poor initial interactions
> for dyads composed of 2 'disagreeables.'"

Two points. On Extraversion the *mixed* pair was the worse pair, the opposite of
the complementarity intuition. On Agreeableness, what hurts is two low-A people,
not sameness as such; a high-A/high-A pair was fine. That is "more A is better",
not "A needs both poles".

## 5. The counter-case for Extraversion, stated fairly

There is a real, principled literature behind treating Extraversion differently
from Agreeableness. It is the only honest theoretical foothold Cèrcol has, and it
does not say what Cèrcol currently says.

### 5.1 The circumplex complementarity rule

The rule is that interpersonal behaviour is complementary when it is **similar on
affiliation/warmth** and **opposite on control/dominance**. It originates with
Carson (1969) and Kiesler (1983).

> Kiesler, D. J. (1983). The 1982 Interpersonal Circle: a taxonomy for
> complementarity in human transactions. *Psychological Review*, 90(3), 185–214.
> https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.90.3.185

**Kiesler (1983) itself could not be opened** (section 9). The rule is quoted
here from a source read in full:

> Tiedens, L. Z., & Fragale, A. R. (2003). Power moves: complementarity in
> dominant and submissive nonverbal behavior. *Journal of Personality and Social
> Psychology*, 84(3), 558–568. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.84.3.558

p. 560, verbatim:

> "Circumplex theorists predict that people's behaviors will be similar to
> interactional partners along the affiliation dimension and opposite along the
> control dimension, and that when this complementary response occurs, the
> partners will like each other more and will be more comfortable (Carson, 1969;
> Horowitz et al., 1991; Kiesler, 1983)."

Tiedens & Fragale's own results, Experiment 1 (N = 93 analysed): postural
response followed complementarity rather than mimicry, F(2, 87) = 3.64, p < .05,
linear contrast F(2, 87) = 7.23, p < .01; liking higher under complementarity
(M = 4.79, SD = 1.23) than mimicry (M = 3.89, SD = 1.14), F(2, 90) = 7.07,
p < .01; comfort M = 5.04 (SD 1.07) vs 4.11 (SD 1.21), F(2, 90) = 5.63, p < .05.
Experiment 2 (N = 74 analysed) replicated: liking interaction F(1, 70) = 4.89,
p < .05, complementary M = 5.01 (SD 0.80) vs mimicry M = 4.56 (SD 0.95),
t(70) = 2.21, p < .05.

**They never manipulated warmth.** The asymmetry is asserted from circumplex
theory, not demonstrated within the paper. Outcomes are dyadic liking and
comfort, not team performance.

### 5.2 The asymmetry replicates behaviourally but not in outcomes

> Markey, P., Lowmaster, S., & Eichler, W. (2010). A real-time assessment of
> interpersonal complementarity. *Personal Relationships*, 17(1), 13–25.
> https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01249.x

66 undergraduates, 33 dyads, 12-minute unstructured interaction, joystick coding.
Warmth profiles correlated positively between partners (mean profile r = .36,
t(32) = 5.88, p < .01); dominance profiles correlated negatively (mean profile
r = −.32, t(32) = −6.63, p < .01); pseudo-dyad controls r = .04 and .03. The
behavioural asymmetry is real.

But only **warmth** complementarity predicted outcomes:

| Outcome | Dominance complementarity | Warmth complementarity |
|---|---|---|
| Liking | b = −.46, SE = .78, t(31) = −0.47, p = .44 (r = −.02) | b = 1.05, SE = .47, t(31) = 2.21, p < .05 (r = .36) |
| Task mistakes | r = −.13, ns | r = −.37, p < .05 |
| Task speed | r = −.17, ns | r = −.34, p < .05 |

They also cite the standing critique (Orford, 1986) that support for
complementarity has been found on warmth while dominance repeatedly fails to
induce submission.

> Halberstadt, A. L., Pincus, A. L., Mogle, J., & Ansell, E. B. (2023).
> Interpersonal complementarity and affect in daily life. *Motivation and
> Emotion*, 47(2), 270–281. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-022-10003-0

N = 227, 14,870 interactions over 21 days. Communal (warmth) complementarity
β = 0.42, p < .0001, standardised difference 0.67; agentic (dominance)
complementarity β = 0.07, p = .0008, standardised difference 0.13. The warmth
effect is roughly six times the dominance effect.

**Reading.** The circumplex tradition gives a genuine reason to treat E and A
differently, but the direction is the reverse of Cèrcol's. The well-supported
half is *warmth reciprocity*: similarity on A is good. The *dominance
complementarity* half is the contested one that repeatedly fails to predict
outcomes. Cèrcol currently treats E and A identically as balance dimensions. The
circumplex literature says A should be a similarity dimension and E is the one
where opposites might fit, on thin evidence.

There is **no meta-analysis of interpersonal complementarity**. Do not claim one
exists. The strongest single confirmatory study is Sadler, P., & Woody, E. (2003),
*JPSP* 84(1), 80–96 (PMID 12518972), 112 previously unacquainted mixed-sex pairs,
SEM showing partners eliciting similar affiliation and opposite dominance;
abstract only.

**One more caution.** Extraversion is not dominance. Dominance/assertiveness is
one facet of Extraversion; the trait also carries sociability, positive affect and
excitement-seeking, for which there is no complementarity prediction at all.
Importing a dominance-complementarity argument to license a factor-level design
decision is a facet-to-factor overreach and should be stated as one.

### 5.3 Complementary person-team fit on Extraversion

> Kristof-Brown, A., Barrick, M. R., & Stevens, C. K. (2005). When opposites
> attract: a multi-sample demonstration of complementary person-team fit on
> extraversion. *Journal of Personality*, 73(4), 935–958.
> https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6494.2005.00334.x

Route: verbatim PubMed abstract via E-utilities (PMID 15958140).

> "Unlike most studies of personality-based fit, which emphasize similarity, we
> predicted that complementary fit on extraversion (i.e., high individual-low
> team or low individual-high team levels) would result in greater attraction to
> the team. Data from two independent samples of intact project teams were
> analyzed, including 324 MBA students comprising 64 case analysis teams and 217
> members of 26 manufacturing teams. Using polynomial regression analysis and
> three-dimensional surface plots, our results supported the predicted
> relationship. In addition, the data indicated that individuals who were more
> attracted to their teams were also better performers, as judged by their peers
> and supervisors."

This is the best-evidenced Extraversion-specific complementarity result. But read
what it is: the outcome is an **individual's attraction to their team**, and the
design is **person-to-team fit**, not team composition. It says an introvert is
happier surrounded by extraverts and vice versa. It does not say a team
containing both outperforms a team containing neither. The performance link is
indirect.

### 5.4 Hierarchy studies

> Ronay, R., Greenaway, K., Anicich, E. M., & Galinsky, A. D. (2012). The path to
> glory is paved with hierarchy: when hierarchical differentiation increases
> group effectiveness. *Psychological Science*, 23(6), 669–677.
> https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611433876

Groups of one high-power-primed, one low-power-primed and one baseline member
outperformed all-high and all-low groups on a **procedurally interdependent**
task, with **no effect** on a procedurally independent task; Experiment 2
replicated using prenatal testosterone, with intragroup conflict mediating the
decrement for high-testosterone groups. Abstract only (PubMed); no effect sizes
obtained.

> Groysberg, B., Polzer, J. T., & Elfenbein, H. A. (2011). Too many cooks spoil
> the broth: how high-status individuals decrease group effectiveness.
> *Organization Science*, 22(3), 722–737. https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.1100.0547

Wall Street sell-side equity analysts. Groups benefited from high-status members
up to a point, after which marginal benefit declined and turned negative, most
strongly when stars were concentrated in few sectors. Abstract only.

**Reading.** These are the best team-level results consistent with an
Extraversion-balance intuition, and they are about **status and power**, not the
Extraversion trait. Both are *inverted-U on elevation* results rather than
both-poles-are-necessary results, and Ronay's is task-contingent.

## 6. The variance-versus-mean question generally

> Halfhill, T., Sundstrom, E., Lahner, J., Calderone, W., & Nielsen, T. M.
> (2005). Group personality composition and group effectiveness: an integrative
> review of empirical research. *Small Group Research*, 36(1), 83–105.
> https://doi.org/10.1177/1046496404268538

Route: full PDF hosted at Kellogg,
`https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/uzzi/ftp/teaching%20materials/recent%20articles/groups/Personality_composition_in_groups.pdf`

Abstract, verbatim:

> "A review of 31 studies yielding 334 unique relationships distinguished task
> and relationship predictors and criteria. Findings indicate operational
> definitions of GPC are varied, variance scores correlate negatively with group
> effectiveness, and minimum scores predict as well as mean scores. GPC is
> related to group effectiveness, and the effect is stronger in field studies
> than lab studies."

Table 2 (p. 94), mean and median r by operationalisation across 334
relationships:

| Operationalisation | n of r's | Mean r | Median r |
|---|---|---|---|
| Team mean | 171 | .13 | .15 |
| Team minimum | 46 | .14 | .17 |
| Team maximum | 32 | .05 | .04 |
| **Team variance** | **70** | **−.13** | **−.12** |
| % above mean | 18 | .05 | −.02 |

Discussion, verbatim (p. 97):

> "The most significant, in our opinion, was the negative relationship between
> the variance operationalization and group effectiveness. This implies that
> groups homogenize around certain personality variables, and the more
> heterogeneous the group becomes the more likely performance will decrease."

Conclusions, verbatim (p. 99):

> "it is now clear that variability in GPC is inversely related to group
> effectiveness"

Two caveats in Cèrcol's favour: this is a descriptive vote-count with uncorrected
correlations, not a psychometric meta-analysis; and the −.13 is averaged across
traits, so it cannot by itself rule out a trait-specific exception. But it is the
general prior, and the general prior is against heterogeneity.

### What the four operationalisations mean for Cèrcol

- **Mean/elevation.** Positive and reliable for Agreeableness and
  Conscientiousness. Small and setting-dependent for Extraversion, Emotional
  Stability and Openness.
- **Minimum.** Predicts about as well as the mean (Halfhill mean r = .14; Bell's
  strongest field result is minimum agreeableness ρ = .37). This is a "one bad
  apple" story, and it argues *against* deliberately including the low pole.
- **Maximum.** Weak (Halfhill mean r = .05).
- **Variance.** Negative overall; reliably negative for A and C; positive but
  never significant for E; null-to-negative for O.

**There is no operationalisation in this literature that corresponds to
"representation at both poles."** The closest available construct is variance.
Cèrcol should either adopt variance as the operationalisation of its hypothesis
or say explicitly that it is proposing a new one.

### The largest and only preregistered diversity meta-analysis

> Wallrich, L., Opara, V., Wesołowska, M., Barnoth, D., & Yousefi, S. (2024).
> The relationship between team diversity and team performance: reconciling
> promise and reality through a comprehensive meta-analysis registered report.
> *Journal of Business and Psychology*, 39(6), 1303–1354.
> https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-024-09977-0

Route: full preprint PDF at Birkbeck,
`https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/id/eprint/53930/1/diversity_meta_preprint_updated.pdf`.
**615 reports, 2,638 effect sizes**, the largest and only preregistered analysis
in this literature.

| Domain | k | r [95% CI] | 80% credibility interval |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overall | 2,638 | .024 [.015, .033] | [−.167, .215] |
| Demographic | 1,105 | .014 [.001, .026] | [−.178, .205] |
| Cognitive | 747 | .020 [.001, .039] | [−.171, .212] |
| Job-related | 786 | .042 [.025, .058] | [−.150, .233] |

Verbatim:

> "the average linear relationships between demographic, job-related and
> cognitive diversity, and team performance are significant and positive, but
> insubstantial (|r| < .1)."
>
> "The estimated correlations were significant and insubstantial [...] for
> diversity in gender, nationality, degree, function and tenure, not significant
> and insubstantial for diversity in age, race/ethnicity, educational level and
> values, and **inconclusive for the remaining sub-dimensions (personality and
> intelligence)**."

Two things Cèrcol should take from this. First, even at 615 reports **personality
diversity is "inconclusive"**: there is not enough clean data in the world to
settle it, which is a legitimate reason for Cèrcol to collect some, and a
legitimate reason not to assert an answer in the meantime. Second, the 80%
credibility intervals span roughly [−.17, +.22] in every domain: the average is
near zero because the effect is entirely moderator-contingent. Their one durable
moderator is that correlations were more positive for complex and creative tasks.

Converging estimates tabulated in the same paper: van Dijk et al. (2012)
deep-level r = −.01 ns and job-related +.05 significant; Triana et al. (2021)
deep-level diversity → performance ρ = −.01 and → team conflict ρ = +.14;
Bowers et al. (2000) found ability, personality and gender diversity not
significantly linked to performance.

For completeness on the narrative side:

> van Knippenberg, D., & Schippers, M. C. (2007). Work group diversity. *Annual
> Review of Psychology*, 58, 515–541.
> https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.58.110405.085546

Full publisher PDF read at the Erasmus repository. p. 518, verbatim:

> "In their simplest form (a main effect of diversity), neither analysis is
> supported. Evidence for the positive effects as well as for the negative
> effects of diversity is highly inconsistent."
>
> "It seems time to declare the bankruptcy of the main effects approach and to
> argue for models that are more complex and that consider moderating
> variables."

And Horwitz, S. K., & Horwitz, I. B. (2007). The effects of team diversity on
team outcomes: a meta-analytic review of team demography. *Journal of
Management*, 33(6), 987–1015. https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206307308587, abstract
verbatim: "Support was found for the positive effect of task-related diversity on
performance, although bio-demographic diversity was not significantly related to
performance." Task-related r = .10 across 35 studies and 78 effect sizes, with
the effect stronger for self-reported than manager-rated performance.

**None of this is about personality poles.** The diversity literature that finds
positive effects finds them for *informational and functional* diversity, not for
trait diversity. If Cèrcol wants a "diversity helps" argument it has to be an
informational one, and Cèrcol's roles are not informational.

### Bell et al. (2018) on why the field's operationalisations are impoverished

> Bell, S. T., Brown, S. G., Colaneri, A., & Outland, N. (2018). Team composition
> and the ABCs of teamwork. *American Psychologist*, 73(4), 349–362.
> https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0000305

Route: APA posts the accepted manuscript openly at
`psycnet.apa.org/manuscript/2018-23205-005.pdf`; read in full. Page references
are to the accepted-manuscript pagination.

The most useful sentence for Cèrcol (p. 22): researchers default to simple
descriptive statistics such as the team mean, and doing so is "potentially
masking team composition and teamwork relationships that would otherwise be
observed". They suggest team profile models incorporating **multiple moments of a
distribution (mean, variance, skewness)** in a single operationalisation.

Discovery 8 (pp. 18–20) organises diversity around Harrison & Klein's (2007)
three conceptualisations, **separation, variety, disparity**, each implying a
different index: separation → standard deviation or mean Euclidean distance;
variety → Blau's index; disparity → coefficient of variation. Table 1 (p. 38) is
the operational crosswalk, and it is directly usable by Cèrcol: "Similarity" →
mean + variance, or proportion high or low; "Diversity (separation)" → SD or mean
Euclidean distance; "Requisite KSAOs" → minimum, maximum.

On heterogeneity specifically:

- Extraversion (pp. 10–11): team neuroticism and extraversion diversity
  *decreased* both positive and negative mood convergence in teams, interacting
  with trait susceptibility to mood contagion (citing Sy & Choi, 2013).
- Agreeableness (pp. 14–15): higher team **minimum** scores on agreeableness and
  emotional stability were positively related to team cohesion, while more team
  **variability** on these traits was negatively related to team cohesion
  (citing Barrick et al., 1998). Extended with Ferguson & Peterson (2015): MBA
  teams more diverse in propensity to trust (an agreeableness facet) had lower
  perceived similarity, lower initial intra-team trust, and greater relationship
  conflict.
- Openness (p. 19): appears not as a heterogeneity variable at all but as a
  deep-level **moderator** of surface-level diversity (citing Homan et al.,
  2008).

**This is the paper that gives Cèrcol its best constructive move**: not "balance",
but a named, published operationalisation from Harrison & Klein's typology, with
a stated hypothesis about which one applies to which dimension.

## 7. Verdict per dimension

### Presence / Extraversion: NOT SUPPORTED, but not refuted either

**Strongest source for:** Prewett, Walvoord, Stilson, Rossi & Brannick (2009),
which is the only meta-analysis to build the complementary-fit prediction into
its design for Extraversion specifically, and reports heterogeneity r̄ = +.06.
Backed by Humphrey et al. (2007) and Kristof-Brown et al. (2005), and by the
circumplex dominance-complementarity rule.

**Strongest source against:** Peeters et al. (2006), which tested the hypothesis
directly as H1b and returned ρ = 0.06, 90% CI −0.06 to 0.18, k = 6, N = 332;
plus den Hartog et al. (2020), γ = −0.22, p < .001, and Müller et al. (2024), a
randomised composition experiment favouring homogeneity.

**Reading.** Three meta-analyses put the point estimate on Cèrcol's side of zero
and none of them reaches significance. Barry & Stewart (1997) is a curvilinear
*elevation* result, not a variance result, and its interpretation is contested.
The two most recent team-level tests are negative. This is a live hypothesis with
a weakly favourable prior and real published theory behind it, and it is the only
one of the three that can honestly be kept in that form.

### Bond / Agreeableness: CONTRADICTED

**Strongest source:** Peeters et al. (2006), elevation ρ = 0.24 (90% CI 0.09 to
0.39), rising to ρ = 0.51 in professional teams; variability ρ = −0.12 (90% CI
−0.16 to −0.07). Five out of five meta-analytic variance estimates are negative
(Bell −.04, Prewett −.07, Peeters −0.12, Carter −.14, van Dijk −.03).

**Corroborating:** Bell's strongest field result of any kind is **minimum**
agreeableness ρ = .37, i.e. the low pole is the thing to avoid. Bell et al.
(2018, pp. 14–15) report team variability on agreeableness negatively related to
cohesion. The circumplex tradition independently predicts warmth should be
reciprocal, and warmth reciprocity is the strongly supported half of that
literature (Markey et al. 2010 liking r = .36; Halberstadt et al. 2023 β = 0.42).
Cuperman & Ickes (2009) add that two low-A people is the uniquely bad pairing.

**Only contrary datum:** den Hartog's over-time slope, γ = 0.07, p = .035, against
a null main effect, in 32 teams.

There is nothing here that supports needing a low-Bond pole.

### Vision / Openness: NOT SUPPORTED

**Strongest source:** Peeters et al. (2006), elevation ρ = 0.03 (90% CI −0.14 to
0.20, k = 6), variability ρ = −0.01 (90% CI −0.15 to 0.12, k = 4), with the only
significant variability estimate being **−0.11** in professional teams. Peeters
declines to draw any conclusion for this trait. Bell's openness heterogeneity is
r̄ = −.02 and negative in field settings; Driskell et al. (2024, p. 8) note both
Bell and Peeters found openness diversity negatively affecting performance in
field settings. Prewett et al. did not code Openness at all. den Hartog found no
effect. Bell et al. (2018) treat openness as a moderator of surface-level
diversity, not as a diversity variable.

This is the weakest of the three. There is no basis for calling it a balance
dimension, and the one direction that shows up is negative.

**One correction in Cèrcol's favour.** SCIENCE.md line 37 says Bell (2007) found
"team mean openness to experience among the strongest composition predictors of
team performance in field studies". That is **accurate**, and it is in Bell's own
abstract, verbatim (PubMed PMID 17484544):

> "In contrast, team minimum agreeableness and team mean conscientiousness,
> openness to experience, collectivism, and preference for teamwork emerged as
> strong predictors of team performance in field studies."

So the *elevation* claim for Openness stands as written. What does not stand is
using it to justify a **balance** dimension: a strong field effect for team *mean*
openness is an argument for a team having more openness, not for a team needing
both poles. Note also that Bell's own abstract lists only mean, minimum and
maximum among the operationalisations it reports as significant, never variance;
and Peeters et al. could not reproduce the elevation effect (ρ = 0.03,
90% CI −0.14 to 0.20).

### Discipline / Conscientiousness: SUPPORTED as directional-up

**Strongest source:** Peeters et al. (2006), elevation ρ = 0.20 (90% CI 0.09 to
0.31, k = 10, N = 527), rising to ρ = 0.42 (0.33 to 0.51) in professional teams;
variability ρ = −0.24 (90% CI −0.33 to −0.14), the largest variance penalty of
any trait in any meta-analysis. Corroborated by Bell (ρ = .14 mean, −.03
heterogeneity), Prewett (r̄ = .13 mean, −.06 heterogeneity), Carter (r = .08
mean, −.12 heterogeneity), van Dijk (−.09), den Hartog (γ = −0.12, p = .037), and
Humphrey et al. (2007), whose entire design premise was that C variance should be
minimised.

Cèrcol's current text is correct and can be strengthened: it is not only that
high mean C helps, it is that dispersion in C actively hurts. That is a stronger
reason not to make it a balance dimension than the one currently given.

### Depth / Neuroticism: NOT SUPPORTED as directional-down

**Strongest source:** Peeters et al. (2006), Emotional Stability elevation
ρ = 0.04, 90% CI −0.06 to 0.13 (k = 9, N = 497); variability ρ = 0.02, 90% CI
−0.13 to 0.16. In student teams the elevation effect is significant but
*negative* (ρ = −0.04, 90% CI −0.07 to −0.01), slightly favouring lower emotional
stability; in professional teams it is positive and non-significant (ρ = 0.14,
90% CI −0.05 to 0.32). Peeters: "we restrain ourselves from drawing general
conclusions for this trait."

Bell's overall emotional-stability mean is ρ = .13 and Carter's r = .13, so
there is a small positive mean effect in the broader pool, but the direction and
reliability are not what SCIENCE.md currently claims. The line "Bell (2007) finds
a consistent negative effect of high mean N on team cohesion and performance"
overstates a small, setting-dependent effect and attributes to Bell a cohesion
finding I could not locate in any source that reproduces her tables.

The right conclusion is that N is excluded from the role-defining set because
there is no evidence that either pole carries a distinct functional contribution,
not because high N is established as harmful.

## 8. AB5C: what it is and what it licenses

> Hofstee, W. K. B., De Raad, B., & Goldberg, L. R. (1992). Integration of the
> Big Five and circumplex approaches to trait structure. *Journal of Personality
> and Social Psychology*, 63(1), 146–163.
> https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.63.1.146

Route: verbatim abstract from PubMed via NCBI E-utilities (PMID 1494982); IPIP
scale documentation read directly at `https://ipip.ori.org/newAB5CKey.htm`.

Abstract, verbatim:

> "To integrate the 5-dimensional simple-structure and circumplex models of
> personality, the Abridged Big Five Dimensional Circumplex (AB5C) taxonomy of
> personality traits was developed, consisting of the 10 circumplexes that can
> be formed by pitting each of the Big Five factors against one another. The
> model maps facets of the Big Five dimensions as blends of 2 factors. An
> application to data consisting of 636 self-ratings and peer ratings on 540
> personality trait adjectives yielded 34 well-defined facets out of a possible
> 45. The AB5C solution is compared with simple-structure and lower dimensional
> circumplex solutions, and its integrative and corrective potential are
> discussed, as well as its limitations."

The scanned full text was read page by page from Goldberg's own ORI copy at
`https://projects.ori.org/lrg/PDFs_papers/Bigfive-circum-jpsp92.pdf` (a JBIG2
image PDF with no text layer, so read as images).

**What it is.** A lexical taxonomy of *individual-differences trait structure*.
p. 147, verbatim:

> "In this Abridged Big Five Dimensional Circumplex (AB5C), each trait is
> characterized by its loadings on a subset of two of the five factors. There
> are 10 such subsets; therefore, the AB5C model comprises the 10
> two-dimensional circumplexes formed by taking as a base two factors at a
> time."

Each plane is cut into **12 segments of 30°** (Wiggins's 45° octants are
explicitly rejected as too coarse). Assignment rule, p. 147: "Only the two
highest loadings of a variable are considered. If the primary loading is at least
3.73 as large as the secondary loading, the variable is assigned to the
pure-factor segment associated with the primary loading." 90 unipolar segments =
**45 bipolar facets**, of which **34 were well defined** in the data.

**Correction to a factual claim SCIENCE.md may be carrying: the 1992 data are
American, not Dutch.** The authors are at Groningen, but p. 147 states the
empirical application is Goldberg's Oregon data: "We applied the AB5C procedure
to data collected by Goldberg (1990): 320 college students described themselves
using 587 trait-descriptive adjectives, and 316 of these subjects used the same
terms to describe someone of their own age and sex whom they knew well and
liked." 320 + 316 = N = 636 rating profiles; 540 terms after dropping 47
amplifiers. The *Dutch* AB5C is a separate paper (Hofstee & de Raad, 1991,
*Nederlands Tijdschrift voor de Psychologie*, 46, 262–274).

**What it says about teams: nothing.** Every page of running text (146–152,
161–163; 152–160 is the facet-by-facet adjective table) was read. There is no
mention of teams, groups, team functioning, collaboration or organisational
application anywhere. The only applied extension in the paper is Holland's
vocational typology, and it is used to demonstrate the method, not to endorse an
application. p. 161, verbatim:

> "We do not wish to imply that types should fit into one of the boxes of the
> AB5C taxonomy. [...] our intention is not to criticize a particular typology,
> but to demonstrate an analytic procedure that could be applied constructively
> to a variety of models."

And the authors' own limit, p. 161, verbatim:

> "We do not presume that the AB5C model is definitive or comprehensive. Rather,
> it is demonstrably procrustean in that it discards any variance that is not
> covered by the first two principal components, and it does not deal adequately
> with those few variables that load highly on more than two of these factors."

**What IPIP publishes.** Two pages, both read directly:
`https://ipip.ori.org/newAB5CKey.htm` ("The Items in the 45 Preliminary IPIP
Scales Measuring the 45 AB5C Facets") and
`https://ipip.ori.org/newAB5CTable.htm` ("Characteristics of the 45 Preliminary
IPIP Scales Targeted at the AB5C Facets"). The 45 scales exist, 9 per factor

### Precedent for deriving team roles from a personality circumplex

**None found that uses Big Five factors as the axes.** The published team-role
literature derives roles from *behavioural* taxonomies, not from trait geometry.

The most rigorous modern instrument is Mumford, T. V., Van Iddekinge, C. H.,
Morgeson, F. P., & Campion, M. A. (2008). The Team Role Test: development and
validation of a team role knowledge situational judgment test. *Journal of
Applied Psychology*, 93(2), 250–267.
https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.93.2.250 (verbatim abstract via PubMed
PMID 18361630; full text read from the author's copy at morgeson.com, whose TLS
certificate has expired, so fetched with `curl -k`). Its 10 roles come from
Mumford et al. (2006), who reviewed the literature, extracted **120 team member
roles and Q-sorted them into 10** (p. 252). The lineage table (Table 1, p. 252)
traces each role to Benne & Sheats (1948), Bales (1950), Belbin (1993), Ancona &
Caldwell (1992), McCann & Margerison (1995), Barry (1991), DuBrin (1995) and
Parker (1996). None derives roles from a Big Five circumplex, and the instrument
measures role *knowledge* by situational judgement, not trait position. Validity:
r = .34 (N = 93 academic project teams) and r = .30 (N = 82 work teams), with
incremental validity over mental ability and the Big Five.

**There is no Mumford team-role circumplex.** Search engines confidently
attribute one to Mumford, Campion & Morgeson (2006). The sentence they attribute
is verbatim from Nestsiarovich & Pons (2020) below, which cites the Mumford
chapter only for the 120 → 10 → 3 taxonomy. Do not cite a Mumford circumplex.

Four genuine near-precedents, in order of how much they matter to Cèrcol:

1. **Driskell, T., Driskell, J. E., Burke, C. S., & Salas, E. (2017). Team
   roles: a review and integration. *Small Group Research*, 48(4), 482–511.
   https://doi.org/10.1177/1046496417711529** Abstract verbatim: "we attempt to
   integrate this research by deriving a model that describes three primary
   behavioral dimensions that underlie team role behavior: (a) dominance, (b)
   sociability, and (c) task orientation. Based on this model, we conduct a
   cluster analysis of the 154 team roles described in previous research. We
   identify 13 primary team role clusters." **This is the paper most likely to
   pre-empt Cèrcol's structural claim.** Dominance and sociability are the
   interpersonal circumplex axes renamed; task orientation is close to
   Conscientiousness; and 13 clusters is close to 12 roles. Full text is
   paywalled and was **not obtained**. Get it through a library before
   SCIENCE.md claims novelty *or* claims a precedent.
2. **Nestsiarovich, K., & Pons, D. (2020). Team role adoption and distribution
   in engineering project meetings. *Behavioral Sciences*, 10(2), 57.
   https://doi.org/10.3390/bs10020057** (MDPI open access, read in full).
   Section 4.2 is titled "Model: Circumplex of Team Roles" and places **twelve
   roles** as segments around two axes, Personal Agency ↔ Communion and Social
   Engagement ↔ Disengagement. Verbatim (p. 17): "Each point within the
   circumplex represents a weighted mixture of Personal agency/Communion and
   Social Engagement." The axes are *behavioural*, not Big Five factors;
   personality enters only as a correlate. Their own future-work section says
   "Other personality traits (Extroversion, Conscientiousness, Openness to

   leave Cèrcol's move explicitly open. Evidence base is thin: 5 student teams,
   qualitative, New Zealand.
3. **Redeker, M., de Vries, R. E., Rouckhout, D., Vermeren, P., & de Fruyt, F.
   (2014). Integrating leadership: the leadership circumplex. *European Journal
   of Work and Organizational Psychology*, 23(3), 435–455.
   https://doi.org/10.1080/1359432X.2012.738671** (read in full). Eight named
   octant styles on agency × communion, validated with MDS and CIRCUM, with the
   axes explicitly tied to "the two interpersonal Big Five factors which have
   been found to be most closely aligned to the two circumplex dimensions
   agency/control and communion/affiliation." This is the best *methodological*
   template for what Cèrcol is doing: named applied roles as two-factor blends,
   properly validated. It covers one plane, not ten.
4. **McCann & Margerison's Team Management Wheel** (as described in Mumford et
   al. 2008, p. 252): "a model containing eight roles located on four dimensions,
   which the researchers called relationships (extroversion–introversion),
   information (practical–creative), decision making (analytical–beliefs), and
   organization (structured–flexible)." Structurally the same move Cèrcol makes.
   Mumford et al. class it among the "practitioner-oriented team role

   with that. A precedent, but not one to lean on.

Also relevant, and a caution about role counts: Mathieu, J. E., Tannenbaum,
S. I., Kukenberger, M. R., Donsbach, J. S., & Alliger, G. M. (2015). Team role
experience and orientation. *Group & Organization Management*, 40(1), 6–34.
https://doi.org/10.1177/1059601114562000 (read in full) develops six roles and
positions them as *not* reducible to Big Five blends: "the TREO measures are
distinguishable from, yet generally showed the expected pattern of correlations
with, measures of the Big 5 personality dimensions." They also write that they
"doubt whether there are 10+ empirically distinguishable team roles", which
bears directly on Cèrcol's 12.

On Belbin: Aritzeta, A., Swailes, S., & Senior, B. (2007). Belbin's team role
model: development, validity and applications for team building. *Journal of
Management Studies*, 44(1), 96–118.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6486.2007.00666.x is a narrative review of 43
studies; full text not obtained. Its conclusion, quoted in Mathieu et al. (2015):
Belbin's model has "adequate convergent validity. However, strong associations
between some team roles are observed, indicating weak discriminant validity."
The actual Belbin-to-Big-Five mapping source is Broucek & Randell (1996), *JOOP*
69(4), 389–405, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8325.1996.tb00625.x, and it is
**one role per factor**, the opposite of a two-factor blend. Fisher, Hunter &
Macrosson (1998) ran MDS on roughly 1,796 UK managers and recovered **two
clusters, task and relationship**, not five factors. In no Belbin work does a
role correspond to a two-factor blend, and the mapping was always done after the
fact, which is the reverse of what Cèrcol does.

### Conclusion for SCIENCE.md

Using the AB5C to define team roles is **Cèrcol's own extension.** SCIENCE.md's
line "The AB5C structure is the published scientific framework — we are applying
it, not inventing it" is true about the *structure* and misleading about the
*application*. The honest form is: the trait geometry is published and we are
applying it; the mapping from trait geometry to team roles is ours, and the
authors of the AB5C say of their own model that they "do not presume [it] is
definitive or comprehensive."

**The one place Cèrcol has real published cover and is not claiming it** is the
four Presence × Bond roles (Dolphin, Wolf, Elephant, Owl). Those are the four
quadrants of the interpersonal circumplex, which McCrae & Costa (1989),
https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.56.4.586 (verbatim abstract via PubMed
PMID 2709308, N = 315 adults, Wiggins's revised IAS jointly factored with the
NEO-PI across self, peer and spouse ratings) showed "is defined by the two
dimensions of Extraversion and Agreeableness, and that the circular ordering of
variables is not an artifact of response biases or cognitive schemata." The IPC's
octants are named interpersonal *styles*: the nearest published thing to "roles
as intersections of two Big Five dimensions", and it exists only for the E×A
plane. The Presence × Vision and Bond × Vision roles have no comparable
precedent.

### 9.1 The verdict, plainly

Narrowing means keeping "requires both poles" and restricting it to some subset

for Openness, because there is no signal to narrow.

**But "replace" does not mean "abandon the 12 roles."** The role system does not
depend on the balance premise being true. It depends on the roles being
distinguishable positions in a well-established trait space, which the AB5C
supplies. What the balance premise supplies is a *story* about why those three
axes and why both poles. That story can be rebuilt honestly, and the rebuilt
version is stronger than the current one because it is checkable.

Note also that the balance premise is currently in direct contradiction with
SCIENCE.md's own disclaimer. Line 58 says the system does not claim "that roles
predict team performance." A both-poles *requirement* is a performance claim.
Removing it makes the document internally consistent for the first time.

### 9.2 What a design hypothesis grounded in something looks like

A hypothesis grounded in nothing: "these three dimensions require balance." No
named construct, no operationalisation, no prior, no test, no failure condition.

A hypothesis grounded in something has five properties, and Cèrcol can have all
five:

1. **It names the construct in the field's own vocabulary.** Not "balance" but
   **within-team variance**, or better, one of Harrison & Klein's three
   conceptualisations as laid out in Bell et al. (2018): separation, variety or
   disparity. Cèrcol's claim is a *separation* claim, operationalised as standard
   deviation or mean Euclidean distance.
2. **It states the prior it is going against, with numbers.** The prior is
   negative: Halfhill et al. mean r = −.13 across 70 relationships; Peeters
   ρ = −0.12 (A) and −0.24 (C). Saying so is what makes it a hypothesis rather
   than an assertion, and it is disarming rather than damaging.
3. **It distinguishes the dimensions instead of lumping them.** The literature
   already does: Prewett et al. (2009) built their design on the prediction that
   Extraversion follows *complementary* fit while Agreeableness, Conscientiousness
   and Emotional Stability follow *supplementary* fit. Cèrcol currently treats
   E, A and O identically, which no source does.
4. **It says why these axes and not the others, on grounds that are not a
   performance claim.** Two are available and both are citable:
   - **The interpersonal circumplex.** McCrae & Costa (1989) established that
     "the interpersonal circumplex is defined by the two dimensions of
     Extraversion and Agreeableness". If the object of a role system is *how a
     person relates to others in a group*, then E and A are not an arbitrary
     pick; they are the two axes of the published structure of interpersonal
     behaviour, and their four quadrants are the classical interpersonal
     octant/quadrant scheme, not a Cèrcol invention. This grounds the P×B roles
     directly and is a much better foundation than the AB5C alone.
   - **Exclusion by known direction.** C has an unambiguous directional effect at
     both individual and team level and the largest dispersion penalty of any
     trait, so a role defined by its low pole would be a role defined by a known
     deficit. N has no reliable team-level effect in either direction, so a role
     built on it would have no content. Neither exclusion requires claiming that
     E, A or O need balance.
5. **It specifies what would refute it.** Cèrcol will have team data. State the
   test in advance, with the direction the literature predicts.

That reframing costs Cèrcol nothing it actually had, and it turns the weakest
section of SCIENCE.md into the most rigorous one.

**One thing Cèrcol should stop doing either way**: describing the low pole of
Bond as something a team *needs*. Bell's strongest field finding of any kind is
that team **minimum** agreeableness predicts performance at ρ = .37. The
literature's clearest single message about Agreeableness is that the low pole is
the thing to avoid. Wolf, Owl and Fox can stay as descriptions of where a person
sits; the copy that says a team needs them for balance cannot.

### 9.3 Replacement for "### Why three balance dimensions" (lines 88–105)

```markdown
### Why three role-defining dimensions, and why both poles

Cèrcol builds roles from both poles of three OCEAN dimensions: Presence (E),
Bond (A) and Vision (O). **This is a design decision about the role system, not
a claim about what makes teams perform.** The team composition literature does
not support a both-poles requirement for any Big Five factor, and for two of
these three it points the other way. The evidence is set out here so the
decision can be judged, and refuted, on the record.

**What the literature measures.** Team composition research uses four
operationalisations of a trait within a team: the team mean (elevation), the
team minimum, the team maximum, and the team variance (variability). None of
them corresponds to "representation at both poles"; the closest construct is
variance. Peeters, Van Tuijl, Rutte and Reymen (2006) meta-analysed elevation
and variability against team performance:

| Factor | Elevation ρ (90% CI) | Variability ρ (90% CI) |
|--------|----------------------|------------------------|
| Extraversion | 0.04 (−0.05, 0.13) | 0.06 (−0.06, 0.18) |
| Agreeableness | 0.24 (0.09, 0.39) | −0.12 (−0.16, −0.07) |
| Conscientiousness | 0.20 (0.09, 0.31) | −0.24 (−0.33, −0.14) |
| Emotional stability | 0.04 (−0.06, 0.13) | 0.02 (−0.13, 0.16) |
| Openness | 0.03 (−0.14, 0.20) | −0.01 (−0.15, 0.12) |

k ranges from 4 to 10 studies, N from 222 to 527 teams. Halfhill, Sundstrom,
Lahner, Calderone and Nielsen (2005) reach the same conclusion across 31 studies
and 334 predictor-criterion relationships: variance operationalisations average
r = −.13 against group effectiveness, while mean and minimum operationalisations
average r = .13 and r = .14. Bell (2007) reports the same pattern, with team
minimum agreeableness (ρ = .37 in field settings) among her strongest
predictors.

**So the honest summary is: within-team variance is on average mildly harmful,
and reliably harmful for Agreeableness and Conscientiousness.**

**Where each dimension actually stands.**

- **Presence (E)** is the one open question. Three meta-analyses put the
  variance-performance estimate slightly above zero (Bell 2007, r̄ = +.03;
  Prewett et al. 2009, r̄ = +.06; Peeters et al. 2006, ρ = +0.06) and none of
  them reaches significance. There is a published theoretical reason to expect
  it: Prewett et al. built their design on the prediction that Extraversion
  follows complementary fit while the other factors follow supplementary fit,
  and the interpersonal circumplex holds that dominance is complementary while
  warmth is reciprocal. Barry and Stewart (1997) found the proportion of
  extraverted members related curvilinearly to task focus and group performance
  in 61 self-managed groups. Against it: den Hartog, Runge, Reindl and Lang
  (2020) found team variance in Extraversion negatively related to team
  innovation (γ = −0.22, p < .001, 32 teams), and Müller, Röpke, Konert and
  Bellhäuser (2024) randomly assigned 180 students to groups homogeneous or
  heterogeneous in Extraversion and found the homogeneous groups reported higher
  group work satisfaction. **Cèrcol's position: unresolved, and Cèrcol's own
  data is a legitimate contribution to resolving it.**

- **Bond (A)** points the other way and Cèrcol does not claim otherwise. Higher
  team mean Agreeableness and lower variance both predict better performance,
  in every meta-analysis that has estimated them. The interpersonal circumplex
  makes the same prediction: warmth is reciprocated, not complemented. Cèrcol
  uses both poles of Bond to *describe* where a person sits and what they
  therefore move in a team, not to claim a team performs better for having a
  low-Bond member.

- **Vision (O)** shows no variance effect in either direction. Bell (2007) does
  report team mean openness among the strong predictors of team performance in
  field studies, but that is an argument for a team having more openness, not
  for a team needing both poles, and Peeters et al. could not reproduce even the
  elevation effect (ρ = 0.03, 90% CI −0.14 to 0.20). Where openness *variance*
  is significant at all, in field settings (Bell) and professional teams
  (Peeters, ρ = −0.11), it is negative. Cèrcol uses both poles of Vision to
  describe position, not to claim a team performs better for the spread.

**Why both poles anyway.** Because Cèrcol is not predicting performance. A role
system that admitted only the favourable pole of each dimension would be a
ranking wearing the costume of a taxonomy: everyone would be told they are more
or less of one good thing. Using both poles of three dimensions makes each role
a description of a position, with no implied ordering. E and A are the two axes
of the interpersonal circumplex (McCrae & Costa 1989), which is the published
structure of how people relate to one another in groups, so the four P×B
quadrants are not an arbitrary pick. Vision is the third axis because
Conscientiousness and Neuroticism are unavailable for the reasons in the next
section.

**What would refute this.** Cèrcol collects team-level data. From N ≥ 300
individuals in identifiable teams, the pre-registered test is whether within-team
variance in each of E, A and O predicts self-rated team functioning, controlling
for the team mean on the same trait. The literature predicts a positive but
non-significant coefficient for E, a negative coefficient for A, and a null for
O. If Cèrcol's data reproduce that, the role descriptions that say a team
*needs* both poles must be rewritten as descriptions of what each pole
contributes, without the claim that its absence costs the team anything.
```

## 11. Bonus: things that complicate nearby claims

### 11.1 The Mohammed & Angell DOI in circulation is wrong

`doi:10.1002/job.267` is **not** Mohammed & Angell. The correct record, verified
via Crossref, is:

> Mohammed, S., & Angell, L. C. (2004). Surface- and deep-level diversity in
> workgroups: examining the moderating effects of team orientation and team
> process on relationship conflict. *Journal of Organizational Behavior*, 25(8),
> 1015–1039. https://doi.org/10.1002/job.293

Its deep-level diversity variables are **time urgency and extraversion**, not
agreeableness.

### 11.2 There are two different Halfhill 2005 papers

Halfhill, Sundstrom, Lahner, Calderone & Nielsen (2005), *Small Group Research*
36(1), 83–105, doi:10.1177/1046496404268538 (the integrative review quoted here),
versus Halfhill, Nielsen, Sundstrom & Weilbaecher (2005), *Military Psychology*
17(1) (47 intact military teams). The variance finding belongs to the first.
SCIENCE.md's reference list should be checked for which one it cites.

### 11.3 Individual-level "too much of a good thing"

> Curşeu, P. L., Ilies, R., Vîrgă, D., Maricuţoiu, L., & Sava, F. A. (2019).
> Personality characteristics that are valued in teams: not always "more is
> better"? *International Journal of Psychology*, 54(5), 638–649.
> https://doi.org/10.1002/ijop.12511

Route: full text open at PMC6767192.

Study 1 (N = 220, egg-drop creative task, groups of 3–6), quadratic terms for
peer-rated contributions to teamwork: Extraversion² γ = −.38 (SE .18, p < .05),
Agreeableness² γ = −.41 (SE .18, p < .05), Conscientiousness² γ = −.32 (SE .11,
p < .05), Openness² γ = −.41 (SE .20, p < .05), Neuroticism² γ = −.07 (SE .12,
ns). Study 2 (N = 314, Dutch, collaborative learning, groups of 3–7):
Extraversion² γ = −.14 (SE .04, p < .05), Conscientiousness² γ = −.15 (SE .04,
p < .05); Agreeableness², Openness² and Neuroticism² non-significant.

This is an **individual-level** result: moderate scorers are rated as
contributing most, replicated across two cultures for E and C. It supports
"extremes are not optimal", which cuts against Cèrcol's ±1.0 z centroids as ideal
types. It does not support a team-level both-poles claim.

### 11.4 A faultline meta-analysis in wide circulation has been retracted

Thatcher, S. M. B., & Patel, P. C. (2011). Demographic faultlines: a
meta-analysis of the literature. *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 96(6),
1119–1139. Two things, both verified at PubMed PMID 21688882:

1. The DOI commonly given for it, `10.1037/a0022846`, is wrong. The correct DOI
   is `10.1037/a0024167`.
2. **It was retracted.** Retraction notice, verbatim from the PubMed record:
   "'Demographic faultlines: A meta-analysis of the literature': Retraction of
   Thatcher and Patel (2011). J Appl Psychol. 2016 Aug;101(8):1150. doi:
   10.1037/apl0000137." Retracted for significant errors in Tables 1, 2 and 3
   which may affect the conclusions.

Do not cite it. Its effect sizes came from the exact tables found to be
erroneous. Given the shared author pair, treat the 2012 *Journal of Management*
companion's aggregation cautiously as well.

### 11.5 Cèrcol's own blog is being returned as evidence

During searching, `cercol.team/blog/how-to-read-a-big-five-personality-report/`
was surfaced by a search engine and summarised back as though it were
literature. Cèrcol's own published content is now in the retrieval pool that
research on Cèrcol's claims draws from. Keep it out of any citation chain, and
be aware that anything Cèrcol asserts publicly will come back as apparent
corroboration.
