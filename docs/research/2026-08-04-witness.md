# Literature grounding for the Cèrcol Witness instrument

Scope: the five questions in the brief, against the instrument as shipped in
`src/utils/witness-scoring.js` and `src/data/witness-adjectives.js`, ADR
0019, the Witness sections of SCIENCE.md, and the four live blog articles
that make quantitative claims about it.

Every DOI below was resolved through the Crossref API
(`https://api.crossref.org/works/{doi}`) and returned the expected title,
journal, volume, issue and pages. The retrieval route is stated per source.
Where a source was read as page images because the PDF has no text layer,
that is said explicitly.

**What the instrument actually is, for the record.** Thirteen blocks. Each
block shows five trait adjectives, one per Big Five factor, all five drawn
from the same *pole*. The rater names a best fit, a second best and a worst
fit: a partial ranking of five objects in which ranks 1, 2 and 5 are
observed and ranks 3 and 4 are left tied. Scoring is classical and
summative: `votes[F] += RANK_WEIGHT × valence`, weights
`{best: +1, second: +0.5, worst: −1}`, then
`score[F] = clamp(3 + (votes[F]/13) × 2, 1, 5)`. The corpus is 100
adjectives, verified as exactly 10 per factor per pole. The polarity
sequence sliced to 13 is `P P N P P P N P P N P P P`: ten positive blocks
and three negative, 77 percent positive. Because there are exactly ten
positive markers per factor, the ten positive blocks exhaust the positive
pool; the three negative blocks sample 3 of the 10 negative markers per
factor. A single session therefore shows all 50 positive markers and 15 of
the 50 negative ones.

One structural fact that turns out to matter repeatedly below, and which is
not written down anywhere in the project: **because `POSITIVE_POLE_SIGN`
maps N to `'-'`, a positive block is E+, A+, C+, O+ and N−.** Four
positively keyed adjectives and one negatively keyed one. The blocks are
*uniform in pole* but *mixed in keying*.

## Findings

### 1. Forced-choice measurement: what the design gets right, and what it gets wrong

**1a. Classical scoring of forced-choice blocks is ipsative, and Cèrcol's
rule is the textbook example.**

Brown, A., & Maydeu-Olivares, A. (2011). Item response modeling of
forced-choice questionnaires. *Educational and Psychological Measurement*,
71(3), 460-502. https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164410375112

Route: the author accepted manuscript is open at the Kent Academic
Repository, `https://kar.kent.ac.uk/29626/1/IRT_modeling_of_FC_tests_FINAL.pdf`.
The publisher copy and the KAR landing page both fail on a TLS chain error
through a normal fetch; `curl -k` retrieves the PDF, which was then text
extracted locally. All passages below were read from that file.

> "Typically, rank orders of items in a block are reversed and then added to
> their respective scales to make up a scale score. Therefore relative
> positions of items are treated as absolute."
>
> "It is easy to see that regardless of choices made, the same number of
> points is distributed between items in a block, and the total score on the
> questionnaire is therefore the same (constant) for everyone. ... Therefore,
> although trait scores will vary from individual to individual, their sum
> will remain constrained, making it impossible to score above or below
> average on all scales."

The paper also names Cèrcol's response format and classifies it:

> "It is easy to see that this format type provides an incomplete ranking,
> because it only assigns the first and the last ranks."
>
> "In the case of partial rankings, such as ones observed using the 'most
> like me' - 'least like me' format when n > 3, the information for some
> binary outcomes is missing by design."

Cao and Drasgow define ipsative scoring in a way that matches
`computeWitnessScores` line for line: "responding 'least like me' to
positive statements or 'most like me' to negative statements will be given
−1 point." That is `RANK_WEIGHT.worst × valence` on a positive-valence
adjective and `RANK_WEIGHT.best × valence` on a negative one.

**1b. A block size of five is one above the published ceiling.**

Same paper, Discussion:

> "cognitive load as there are n(n−1)/2 binary comparisons to be performed
> in a block of n items. In practice, blocks of 4 items are probably the
> upper limit for forced-choice tests."

Cèrcol uses five, which is 10 comparisons per block. No published simulation
in this literature uses blocks of five. Brown and Maydeu-Olivares stop at
four and call it the ceiling; Bürkner et al. use triplets only and name
larger blocks as an open question; Hontangas et al. use quads and explicitly
call for "more than four items" to be explored. The design sits outside the
published simulation grid, and the documentation should say so rather than
imply the published results transfer.

**1c. Cèrcol's blocks are mixed-keyed. This is the best unclaimed feature of
the design, and it is undocumented and unguarded.**

The single strongest design recommendation in this literature is that blocks
must not consist only of same-keyed items. Brown and Maydeu-Olivares (2011),
Discussion, subsection "Keyed direction of items":

> "When the forced-choice design produces binary outcomes from comparing
> items from different traits keyed in the same direction, and approximately
> the same number of binary outcomes from comparing items keyed in opposite
> directions, the trait recovery can be good with any number of traits, and
> any trait correlations. This is because items keyed in the same direction
> contribute to the measurement of the difference between 2 trait scores;
> and items keyed in opposite directions contribute to the measurement of
> the sum of the 2 traits involved. When information on the sums and
> differences of the traits involved is available, trait scores can be
> determined accurately because their absolute value can be located in the
> traits' continuum."

And the negative case:

> "Our simulation studies show that when assessing only two traits,
> positively keyed items on their own cannot recover the absolute latent
> trait scores."

Bürkner, P.-C., Schulte, N., & Holling, H. (2019). On the statistical and
practical limitations of Thurstonian IRT models. *Educational and
Psychological Measurement*, 79(5), 827-854.
https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164419832063

Route: Europe PMC full-text XML, PMC6713979; preprint at `psyarxiv.com/dbwn8`.

> "According to extensive simulations, measuring up to five traits using
> blocks of only equally keyed items does not yield sufficiently accurate
> trait scores and inter-trait correlation estimates, neither for frequentist
> nor for Bayesian estimation methods. As a result, persons' trait scores
> remain partially ipsative and, thus, do not allow for valid comparisons
> between persons."

At first glance Cèrcol looks like the failing case, because `ROUND_POLARITY`
makes every block uniform in pole. **It is not.** A positive block is E+,
A+, C+, O+ and N−: four positively keyed items and one negatively keyed one.
Of the 10 pairwise comparisons in the block, **4 are between items keyed in
opposite directions and 6 are not**, and negative blocks have the same
structure (E−, A−, C−, O− plus N+). Forty percent is short of Brown and
Maydeu-Olivares's "approximately the same number", but it is a long way from
the all-same-keyed design their simulations show failing.

This is directly verifiable in the shipped code rather than argued. If all
five items in a block were keyed the same way, each block would contribute a
constant to the sum of votes and the five domain scores would sum to a fixed
number for every rater. They do not. Running the shipped `buildRounds(13)`
and `computeWitnessScores` over 20,000 uniformly random Witnesses, the sum
of the five domain scores has **mean 15.22, SD 0.74**, 90 percent of
sessions between 14.00 and 16.46, observed extremes 12.31 and 18.31. The
non-zero SD is the sum information, and every unit of it comes from the one
inverted-valence adjective per block.

Two consequences:

1. This should be written down as a design property with a citation, not
   left as a side effect of the Neuroticism convention.
2. It is fragile. Any future change that keys all five adjectives in a block
   the same direction, for example "invert N so that every block is
   uniformly desirable", destroys the only source of normative information
   the instrument has. `api/tests/test_witness_prior.py` already guards the
   reachability of the twelve roles; it should also assert that the sum of
   domain scores is not constant.

**1d. Cao and Drasgow: forcing reduces faking, but not in the two
configurations Cèrcol chose.**

Cao, M., & Drasgow, F. (2019). Does forcing reduce faking? A meta-analytic
review of forced-choice personality measures in high-stakes situations.
*Journal of Applied Psychology*, 104(11), 1347-1368.
https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000414

Route: full-text PDF mirror at
`https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/2019-cao.pdf`. Every figure
below was re-read from that PDF directly. k = 74 substudies, N = 267,586.

Abstract: "Results suggest that the overall score inflation effect size for
FC personality measures is 0.06." And: "FC scales were consistently found to
be more faking-resistant when constructed with statements balanced in social
desirability and with responses scored via a normative approach."

Table 3, format moderator:

| Format | k | d | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| PICK (best only) | 40 | .05 | [.01, .09] |
| **MOLE (most and least)** | **32** | **.37** | **[.25, .49]** |

Difference tested at t(71) = 5.06, p < .05. Cèrcol's format is a MOLE
variant.

Table 3, scoring method moderator:

| Scoring | k | d | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normative (IRT) | 8 | **.00** | [−.02, .02] |
| Ipsative | 31 | **.31** | [.15, .46] |
| Partially ipsative | 18 | **.72** | [.54, .90] |

Normative against ipsative: t = −7.97, p < .01.

Table 3, dimensionality moderator, the one Cèrcol is on the right side of:
unidimensional blocks d = .13 (k = 17), multidimensional blocks d = .03
(k = 56), t(72) = −3.32, p < .05. Cèrcol's blocks are multidimensional by
construction, one adjective per factor.

Desirability moderator: desirability-balanced d = .02, t(55) = −3.22 against
unbalanced. Extremity moderator: matched d = .01 [−.06, .09], unmatched
d = .14 [.04, .24].

By-factor overall inflation (Table 2): Emotional Stability .00, Openness
.00, Extraversion .16, Agreeableness .19, Conscientiousness .23. The 80
percent credibility interval on the overall d = .06 is [−0.13, 0.24], which
contains zero.

**What this means for the blog copy.**
`forced-choice-personality-assessment-more-honest-data` currently asserts
"the weight of psychometric evidence is clear: forced-choice instruments
produce less biased, more differentiating data than Likert scales in
high-stakes assessment contexts", supported by an uncited callout claiming
"forced-choice reduces social desirability bias by 40-60%". The actual
meta-analytic result is d = 0.06, and the benefit concentrates in exactly
the configuration Cèrcol does not use.

Recommended replacement:

> Cao and Drasgow (2019) meta-analysed 74 substudies covering 267,586
> respondents and found an overall score inflation effect of d = 0.06 for
> forced-choice personality measures under high stakes, well below the
> values reported for single-statement measures. The benefit is not
> uniform. Forced-choice scales scored normatively through item response
> theory showed d = 0.00; scales scored ipsatively showed d = 0.31 and
> partially ipsative scales d = 0.72. Scales using the pick-one format
> showed d = 0.05 against d = 0.37 for the most-and-least format.
> Multidimensional blocks, which is what Cèrcol uses, showed d = 0.03
> against d = 0.13 for unidimensional blocks.

One qualification that should go alongside it. This is a *self-report*
faking literature. The Witness is a peer instrument, and the bias a
colleague brings is halo, not self-enhancement. See 1g.

**1e. Partial ranking against full ranking: the recovery figure exists, and
it vindicates ADR 0019's round-design decision.**

Hontangas, P. M., de la Torre, J., Ponsoda, V., Leenen, I., Morillo, D., &
Abad, F. J. (2015). Comparing traditional and IRT scoring of forced-choice
tests. *Applied Psychological Measurement*, 39(8), 598-612.
https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621615585851

Route: full text via PubMed Central, PMC5978493. The Europe PMC
`fullTextXML` endpoint returns empty for this ID; the PMC HTML article page
works.

This is the paper the brief was asking for. It simulates four-item blocks and
scores the *same* underlying rankings three ways: PICK (best only), MOLE
(best and worst) and RANK (full ranking), with traditional scoring of +1 for
best, −1 for worst and 0 for the unselected middles, the direct ancestor of
Cèrcol's weighting. Four dimensions, 5,000 respondents, 18 or 36 blocks,
192 crossed conditions.

Table 2, correlations between true thetas and scores:

| Condition | MOLE (traditional) | RANK (traditional) | MOLE (EAP) | RANK (EAP) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overall | .662 | .669 | .924 | .927 |
| 18 blocks | .646 | .654 | .903 | .906 |
| 36 blocks | .678 | .683 | .945 | .947 |
| All-positive polarity | .575 | .581 | .884 | .887 |
| Mixed polarity | .775 | .786 | .953 | .955 |
| Uncorrelated traits | .767 | .771 | .928 | .930 |
| Traits correlated .5 | .557 | .566 | .906 | .909 |

PICK overall was .575 traditional and .831 EAP.

> "the RANK model was the best performing model (r = .67), but it was only
> slightly better than the MOLE model (r = .66). The goodness of recovery of
> theta for these two models was very similar across all conditions. Table 2
> shows that the difference across rows between the RANK and MOLE
> correlations was always less than .013. Therefore, under the conditions
> considered in the study, these models can be considered equivalent."
>
> "For the four-item blocks, the current practical recommendation is to use
> the MOLE rather than the RANK format because the MOLE task is easier for
> the respondents, and no substantial loss in recovery can be expected."
>
> "More than four items should therefore be explored to determine the
> potential benefits of the RANK over the MOLE format."

**This independently confirms the central measurement decision in ADR
0019.** The ADR's simulation found best + second + worst at 0.851 against a
full graded ranking at 0.848 and chose the partial ranking because it costs
less. Hontangas et al. found the same thing in a published simulation with a
different generative model: MOLE and RANK differ by less than .013, and the
partial ranking is recommended because it is easier on the respondent. The
ADR's conclusion was right, and it no longer has to rest on one author's
simulation.

The ipsativity demonstration in the same paper is worth having: for a
condition with genuinely uncorrelated traits, the traditionally scored
dimensions intercorrelated at a mean of **−.258** (range −.288 to −.214),
against −.023 for EAP estimates and −.006 for the generated thetas.

**1f. Is r = 0.851 plausible? Comparable published figures.**

Against **IRT** recovery, 0.851 is unremarkable. Brown and Maydeu-Olivares
(2011) Simulation 2 used 5 traits and 60 items and reported
first-replication correlations between MAP and true scores of .822 (30
pairs, positive only), .863 (20 triplets, positive only), .87 (15 quads,
positive only), rising to .889, .929 and .94 with mixed keying. Bürkner et
al. (2019) Table 2, 5 uncorrelated traits with 15 equally keyed triplets and
high loadings, gives a reliability of .73, which is a correlation of
**.854**: the closest published analogue to Cèrcol's number by trait count
and block count. It comes with a warning the correlation hides, RMSE of 0.54
standard deviation units and inter-trait correlation bias of −0.22, and the
paper's verdict is blunt: "when only using equally keyed triplets and not too
many traits (5 or less), we get such inaccurate estimation of subjects' trait
scores that we would not recommend the T-IRT model for practical
applications."

Against **traditional weighted** scoring, which is what Cèrcol actually does,
0.851 is high. Hontangas et al. get .662 overall for MOLE with traditional
scoring, .575 in all-positive conditions, .775 with mixed polarity, and reach
.87 only in a stacked best case of 36 blocks, high discrimination, mixed
polarity and independent traits.

Consolidated comparator table:

That is not proof the simulation is wrong. It does mean the number should
carry a caveat, because the most likely explanation for the gap is that the
simulation's model of *how a witness chooses* is closely aligned with the
scoring rule that recovers it, which real witnesses are not. This is the
class of error ADR 0019 already warns about in its opening caution, applied
one level up: the model of the respondent is as load-bearing as the model of
the scoring.

**The number Cèrcol should adopt as a prior instead of a simulated one.**
For an honest external expectation of how well thirteen blocks of five with
weighted-sum scoring will recover a profile, **Hontangas et al.'s .662
overall and .775 for the mixed-polarity condition** are the published
anchors. Cèrcol's blocks are 40 percent opposite-keyed, which puts them
nearer the mixed-polarity row. A defensible published prior is therefore
**r ≈ .70 to .78**, against a simulated .851.

**One derived figure, flagged as derived rather than published.** Brown and
Maydeu-Olivares establish that a block of n items encodes n(n−1)/2 pairwise
outcomes. A block of 5 has 10. Knowing ranks 1, 2 and 5 determines 9 of
them; only the comparison between the two unranked middle items is missing.
A plain MOLE block of 5 (best and worst only) would determine 7 of 10. So
the second-best question buys back 2 of the 3 comparisons that MOLE loses at
this block size. No published simulation covers this specific design, so
this is a structural argument, not an empirical recovery estimate. It does
explain why ADR 0019's three-pick design measured slightly better than the
two-pick one it replaced.

**1g. The best source for the feature as a whole, which nobody has cited.**

Brown, A., Inceoglu, I., & Lin, Y. (2017). Preventing rater biases in
360-degree feedback by forcing choice. *Organizational Research Methods*,
20(1), 121-148. https://doi.org/10.1177/1094428116668036

Route: Semantic Scholar pointed to a Figshare deposit; the author manuscript
PDF was resolved through the Figshare API, article 29741099. The direct
Exeter repository link returns an empty file.

This is a forced-choice partial-ranking instrument in a real peer-assessment
setting, which is precisely Cèrcol's use case and which none of the other
sources covers. N = 4,675 raters, 922 managers as targets, 16 competencies,
160 statements in 40 blocks of four, most/least partial ranking, raters were
bosses, peers, subordinates and self. Every rater completed both a Likert
and a forced-choice version of the same blocks.

Abstract: "Likert ratings were subject to strong response biases, making
even theoretically unrelated competencies correlate highly. ... Forced-choice
rankings modelled with Thurstonian IRT yielded as good construct and
convergent validities as the bias-controlled Likert ratings, and slightly
better rater agreement."

Inter-rater agreement, self-assessments excluded:

| Model | ICC target | ICC perspective |
|---|---|---|
| Single-stimulus Likert | .25 | .32 |
| Likert with modelled common method factor | .31 | .39 |
| Forced choice, Thurstonian IRT | .33 | .41 |

On the summed 16-competency overall appraisal score: Likert .29,
method-controlled Likert .31, forced choice .40. Forced choice beat the
method-controlled Likert model on 12 of 16 competencies.

The paper draws the self-versus-other distinction the Cèrcol blog currently
blurs. In self-assessments the common method factor reflects "selective
overreporting on behaviors associated with those of 'ideal employee'"; in
assessments by others it reflects "indiscriminate overreporting on all
behaviors, due to cognitive bias of exaggerated coherence". And the crucial
asymmetry:

> "Previous research in a low-bias context found the FC format performing
> slightly worse than the SS format (Brown & Maydeu-Olivares, 2013)."

Forced choice wins in other-report because halo is severe there, and loses
on reliability in low-bias self-report. **That is the correct argument for
why the Witness uses forced choice and the self-report instruments do not,
and it is a better argument than the one the blog currently makes.**

**1h. What ipsative scoring costs, quantified.**

Brown, A., & Maydeu-Olivares, A. (2013). How IRT can solve problems of
ipsative data in forced-choice questionnaires. *Psychological Methods*,
18(1), 36-52. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030641

Route: full PDF at `https://www.ub.edu/gdne/documents/ipsative_data.pdf`
(Universitat de Barcelona); the Kent Academic Repository record 30530 is the
other route.

> "Ipsative scoring distorts individual profiles (it is impossible to
> achieve all high or all low scale scores), construct validity (covariances
> between scales must sum to zero), criterion related validity (validity
> coefficients must sum to zero), and reliability estimates."

Empirical application, the CCSQ 7.2: 16 traits, 40 blocks of four,
most/least partial ranking, all items positively keyed, N = 610. Table 2:

| Scoring | Range | Median |
|---|---|---|
| Single-stimulus classical, alpha | .78 to .91 | .84 |
| Single-stimulus IRT, empirical reliability | .76 to .91 | .85 |
| Forced-choice classical (ipsative), alpha | .57 to .80 | **.72** |
| Forced-choice IRT, empirical reliability | .72 to .89 | **.80** |

Average inter-scale correlation: normative .22, ipsative .07, IRT
forced-choice .12, IRT single-stimulus .21.

That is the size of the prize for the Thurstonian IRT work ADR 0019 already
lists as a future research project: roughly .08 of reliability on a 40-block
instrument.

The same paper is also the citation for a claim the blog makes but does not
source, and it is the one that most justifies the Witness existing:

> "The fact that respondents cannot endorse all items eliminates
> acquiescence responding (Cheung & Chan, 2002) and will typically result in
> a greater differentiation of ratings given to other persons."
>
> "Bartram (2007) showed that when forced-choice formats are employed in
> assessments of workplace behaviors by line managers, where the halo
> effects are notoriously high, correlations with related personal
> attributes increase by as much as 50% in comparison to single-stimulus
> behavior ratings."

### 2. Adjective-based Big Five measurement: canonical, except on one factor where it cannot be

**2a. Twenty markers per factor with a 10:10 keying split is exactly
Goldberg's design, and the rationale is published.**

Goldberg, L. R. (1992). The development of markers for the Big-Five factor
structure. *Psychological Assessment*, 4(1), 26-42.
https://doi.org/10.1037/1040-3590.4.1.26

Route: the full scanned PDF is in Goldberg's own archive at Oregon Research
Institute,
`https://projects.ori.org/lrg/PDFs_papers/Goldberg.Big-Five-Markers-Psych.Assess.1992.pdf`.
It has no text layer. Pages 32 and 37 were rendered to images with
`pdftoppm` and read directly; every quotation and every number below was
checked against the rendered page, not against a search summary.

Abstract: "A set of 100 unipolar terms proved to be highly robust across
quite diverse samples of self and peer descriptions."

Why 20 per factor, p. 32: "To avoid large differences in reliability between
markers of the five factors, it is reasonable to select the same number of
variables for each of them."

Why 10:10. Saucier (2002, p. 6) restates Goldberg's reason in one sentence:
"To reduce the effect of individual differences in response scale use, 10
items were selected for the positive and 10 for the negative pole of each
factor". That purpose is acquiescence and scale-use control, which matters
for how question 5 resolves.

Table 4, p. 37, coefficient alpha for the five 20-item subsets across six
data sets:

| Sample | I (E) | II (A) | III (C) | IV (ES) | V (I/O) | M |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pooled Raw | .92 | .97 | .94 | .88 | .94 | .93 |
| Pooled Z | .91 | .96 | .94 | .87 | .93 | .92 |
| Liked Raw | .91 | .91 | .91 | .83 | .87 | .89 |
| Liked Z | .90 | .87 | .90 | .82 | .81 | .86 |
| Self Raw | .90 | .88 | .90 | .84 | .85 | .87 |
| Self Z | .90 | .84 | .88 | .83 | .82 | .85 |

Goldberg's caution, p. 37: "Given that these values are so high for 20-item
scales, it is important to discover whether they have been purchased at the
price of attenuated discriminant validities."

Structural robustness, p. 36: "Within the sample of self-descriptions, 100%
of the variables had their highest loadings on the factor for which they had
been selected as markers, and in the sample of peer descriptions, 97% of the
variables passed this test."

Convergence with the NEO-PI domain scales, Table 6, N = 171: Extraversion
.69, Agreeableness .56, Conscientiousness .67, Neuroticism −.69, Openness
.46.

**2b. The 10:10 split is not achievable on Neuroticism, and Cèrcol has done
it anyway.**

This is the sharpest item-level finding in this report. Goldberg, p. 32,
verbatim from the rendered page:

> "whereas there are roughly similar frequencies of adjectives associated
> with each of the two poles of four of the factors, there are extremely few
> terms marking the desirable pole of Factor IV."
>
> "in contrast with adjectives in general and trait adjectives in
> particular, terms describing symptoms of emotional instability (e.g.,
> *anxious, high-strung, moody, nervous, temperamental, tense, touchy*) have
> no direct antonyms. For this reason, it is quite difficult to find more
> than a half-dozen commonly used adjectives that are univocal markers of
> the positive pole of Factor IV, and if one were to insist on equal numbers
> of markers of each pole of the five factors, one would be limited to 50 or
> 60 variables. To increase marker reliability, it may be necessary to relax
> the limitation on equal representation of the two poles of each factor, if
> only for Factor IV."
>
> "a new set of 100 factor markers, 10 for each pole of all factors other
> than Factor IV, which was marked by **6 positive and 14 negative terms**."

Saucier hit the same wall at a shorter length.

Saucier, G. (1994). Mini-Markers: A brief version of Goldberg's unipolar
Big-Five markers. *Journal of Personality Assessment*, 63(3), 506-516.
https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327752jpa6303_8

Route: full scanned PDF on the author's University of Oregon page; the
instrument sheet at `https://pages.uoregon.edu/gsaucier/gsau41.pdf` carries
the varimax-rotated loading table with an extractable text layer and was
read directly.

> "for Factor IV (Emotional Stability), few positive items showed a strong
> performance, so two positive-pole items were combined with six
> negative-pole items."

Confirmed independently from the loading table: Factor IV positive is
*Unenvious* and *Relaxed* only, against six negative. Factor V is the mirror
image, 6 positive against 2 negative. Only Factors I, II and III are 4:4.

**Cèrcol's N− pole is ten items: stable, composed, resilient, grounded,
unflappable, secure, steady, serene, patient, levelheaded.** Goldberg's six
are *Unenvious, Unemotional, Relaxed, Imperturbable, Unexcitable,
Undemanding*. **The intersection is empty.** Cèrcol has produced ten
positive Emotional Stability markers where the two authors who built this
literature found six and two, and none of Cèrcol's ten is one of theirs.

That is not automatically wrong, but Goldberg names the specific failure
mode and Cèrcol has walked into it. Same page:

> "when antonyms are presented in unipolar format, they are not always
> treated as opposites in subjects' self-descriptions (Goldberg &
> Kilkowski, 1985). Indeed, the unipolar terms in some antonym pairs may
> even be associated with different factors. For example, whereas subjects
> have no difficulty describing targets using the bipolar contrast
> *moody-steady*, in unipolar format the first term is highly associated
> with the negative pole of Factor IV (Emotional Instability), and the
> second term is associated with the positive pole of Factor III
> (Conscientiousness)."

**Cèrcol's corpus contains `moody` on N+ and `steady` on N−.** That is
Goldberg's worked counterexample reproduced item for item, and his finding
is that `steady` marks Conscientiousness, not Emotional Stability. On the
same reasoning `patient` reads as Agreeableness, and `composed` and
`levelheaded` are candidates for Conscientiousness. SCIENCE.md's stated
policy is to absorb cross-loadings "as measurement noise", which is
defensible for AB5C blends but not for a marker whose *primary* loading is
on a different factor.

Recommendation: audit the ten N− markers against Goldberg's six and accept
an unbalanced N pole rather than manufacture markers to reach ten.
Goldberg's instruction is explicit. Because `buildRounds` draws one adjective
per factor per pole per round, an unbalanced N pole is a small code change,
not a redesign. Note the interaction with 1c: N is the factor carrying all
of the instrument's sum information, so a mis-keyed N marker costs more than
a mis-keyed marker anywhere else.

**2c. Where 20 markers per factor sits in the published range.**

| Instrument | Items per factor | Format | Alpha range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goldberg 100 unipolar markers (1992) | 20 | adjective | .81 to .97 over six data sets |
| IPIP Big-Five Factor Markers, 100-item | 20 | statement | .88 to .91 |
| IPIP Big-Five Factor Markers, 50-item | 10 | statement | .79 to .87 |
| Saucier Mini-Markers (1994) | 8 | adjective | .69 to .86 |
| Saucier Ortho-40 (2002) | 8 | adjective | .62 to .92 |

IPIP alphas read directly from `https://ipip.ori.org/newBigFive5broadKey.htm`.
Per factor at 20 items: E .91, A .88, C .88, ES .91, I/O .90. At 10 items:
E .87, A .82, C .79, ES .86, I/O .84.

Twenty is the **upper** end of what published adjective instruments use.
Nothing in this tradition uses more than 20 per factor, and everything
shorter is framed as a reliability sacrifice bought for brevity. Saucier's
Table 3 puts the cost of going from 20 to 8 at roughly .06 to .12 of alpha
per scale; his summary of the trade (1994, p. 514) is that the longer
inventory buys "increased reliability" and "a broader sampling of Big-Five
adjectives beyond those close to the core of the factor", while the shorter
one buys "fewer difficult items" and "lower interscale correlations".

**So the answer to the brief's question is that 20 per factor is neither too
few nor too many. It is the canonical number, and this is the one part of
the design that needs no defending.** The 10:10 split is likewise canonical
on four of the five factors.

Saucier, G. (2002). Orthogonal markers for orthogonal factors: The case of
the Big Five. *Journal of Research in Personality*, 36(1), 1-31.
https://doi.org/10.1006/jrpe.2001.2335

Single-authored; a separate Saucier and Goldberg (2002) book chapter exists
and is a common source of confusion. Route: PDF on the author's Oregon page,
read as page images. His Ortho-40 quantifies what Goldberg's caution was
about: dropping to eight carefully chosen items per factor costs about .10
of alpha and buys about .15 lower correlation per scale pair. His
item-selection note treats keying balance as a constraint to preserve even
while optimising for something else: problem items were removed "while
maintaining balanced keying as much as feasible".

### 4. How many raters

**Cèrcol's current position is contradictory.** `api/main.py:1084` sets
`MIN_WITNESSES_FOR_REPORT = 2`, and `src/pages/WitnessSetupPage.jsx:20` sets
`MAX_WITNESSES = 12`. The blog article
`what-the-cercol-witness-instrument-measures` states "The system requires a
minimum of three Witnesses before displaying aggregate scores, which
prevents de-anonymisation by subtraction." That is false as shipped. Fix the
copy or fix the constant; they cannot both stand.

**The citable Spearman-Brown argument exists and is in Connelly and Ones.**
Direct quotation, p. 1104:

> "If the interrater reliability for a single rater is r̄rr = .45 (as was the
> case for the best information sources, family and friends, rating the best
> trait, Extraversion), five other-raters are needed to produce a composite
> with interrater reliability greater than r̄rr = .80. If the interrater
> reliability for a single rater is a more typical value of r̄rr = .30, 10
> other-raters are needed to produce a composite with interrater reliability
> greater than r̄rr = .80. Thus, these results point to a clear need for
> researchers to collect other-ratings from multiple others to overcome the
> drastic idiosyncrasies of a single other-rater."

This is exactly the prior Cèrcol needs, and it comes with the single-rater
input values rather than an assumed one. Table 3, "Meta-Analysis of
Single-Rater Interrater Reliabilities, by Information Source", work colleague
rows:

| Trait | k | N | observed r̄rr | corrected ρrr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Extraversion | 6 | 1,238 | .37 | .44 |
| Conscientiousness | 18 | 2,400 | .32 | .40 |
| Openness | 5 | 928 | .29 | .36 |
| Agreeableness | 6 | 1,238 | .29 | .37 |
| Emotional Stability | 5 | 682 | .28 | .34 |

Mean single-work-colleague interrater reliability: **.31 observed, .38
corrected.** Connelly and Ones summarise the general picture as "generally
between uncorrected r̄rr = .30 and r̄rr = .45."

`how-many-peer-assessors-do-you-need-reliable-personality-data` builds its
whole table on "a typical single-rater reliability of r = .35", sourced to a
blockquote attributed to Connelly and Ones that reads "Inter-rater
reliability for personality ratings by acquaintances typically falls around
.35-.45". That sentence does not appear in the paper. The paper's sentence is
".30 and .45", the informant category that matches Cèrcol is work

| 1 | .31 |
| 2 | .47 |
| 3 | .57 |
| 4 | .64 |
| 5 | .69 |

**Verdict on "three".** Three is not right, and neither is two. At three work
colleagues the composite reliability is .57, below any conventional floor for
interpreting an individual score. The published guidance in the source
Cèrcol already cites is **10 raters** at the single-rater reliability that
applies here. Six is the smallest number that reaches .70.

Cèrcol's cap of 12 is well chosen and can now be defended from the source: 12
reaches .84, and going to 20 buys .90 at nearly double the cost. The floor of
2 cannot be defended on reliability grounds. It can be defended on anonymity

constraint is asking colleagues at all, but the documentation should stop
implying it is a reliability threshold.

Recommended replacement:

> Connelly and Ones (2010) report a mean interrater reliability of .31
> across the Big Five for a single work colleague rating a target (Table 3),
> and state the consequence directly: at a single-rater reliability of .30,
> ten other-raters are needed to reach a composite reliability above .80.
> Applying the Spearman-Brown formula to .31 gives .47 at two Witnesses, .57
> at three, .69 at five, .82 at ten and .84 at twelve. Six Witnesses is the
> smallest number that clears .70. Below that, read the composite as a
> hypothesis rather than a measurement.

**One caveat that cuts the other way.** All of these figures are for
Likert-type rating scales. Cèrcol's Witness is a forced-choice partial
ranking and its per-rater reliability has never been measured against a real
second rater. The .31 prior is the best available starting point and far
better than an assumed .35, but the first real cohort of two-or-more
Witnesses gives Cèrcol its own empirical r₁, and that number should replace
the borrowed one at the same milestone SCIENCE.md already schedules for the
normative priors. Note also that Brown, Inceoglu and Lin (2017), section 1g,
found forced-choice achieving *higher* inter-rater agreement than Likert in
a 360-degree setting (ICC .33 against .25), so .31 may be conservative for
this format.

**Bonus, and the strongest argument for the feature existing at all.**
Connelly and Ones Study 3, Table 11, predicting job performance:
other-ratings of Conscientiousness reach ρov = .29 against .20 for
self-ratings, with non-overlapping confidence intervals, and other-ratings of
Openness reach .22 against .04. Their conclusion: "The incremental validity
from adding other-ratings to self-ratings was typically substantial. The
converse, however, did not hold: Self-ratings added relatively little
prediction beyond that of other-ratings alone." That is a much stronger claim
than the blog currently makes, it is quotable, and it comes from the paper
Cèrcol already cites.

### 5. Negative-pole blocks and acquiescence: the 75/25 split is not wrong, it is inert

**5a. A forced-choice format does not have an acquiescence problem, so the
50/50 rule does not apply.**

Brown and Maydeu-Olivares (2013), p. 37, quoted in full in 1h:

> "The fact that respondents cannot endorse all items eliminates
> acquiescence responding (Cheung & Chan, 2002) and will typically result in
> a greater differentiation of ratings given to other persons."

An absence finding, verified independently: the strings `acquiesc` and
`yea-say` appear **zero times** in the extracted full text of Cao and
Drasgow's 22-page meta-analysis of forced-choice measurement. Its entire
moderator framework is social desirability, extremity, dimensionality,
format and scoring. Acquiescence is treated as a solved, format-level
non-issue in this literature.

**5b. Every published 50/50 recommendation is scoped to rating scales, and
justified by what it enables: within-person centring.**

Soto, C. J., & John, O. P. (2017). The next Big Five Inventory (BFI-2):
Developing and assessing a hierarchical model with 15 facets to enhance
bandwidth, fidelity, and predictive power. *Journal of Personality and
Social Psychology*, 113(1), 117-143. https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000096

Route: open-access author copy at eScholarship,
`https://escholarship.org/content/qt16x6n05t/qt16x6n05t.pdf` (403s without
browser headers and a Referer).

> "Balancing the number of true-keyed and false-keyed items on each scale
> would clearly distinguish meaningful personality information from
> acquiescence. Specifically, balanced keying would automatically control
> for acquiescence at the scale level, because yea-saying (or nay-saying) in
> response to a scale's true-keyed items would be canceled out by responding
> similarly to its equal number of false-keyed items. Content-balanced
> scales would also allow researchers to easily control for acquiescence at
> the item level by centering each individual's set of item responses around
> their within-person mean."
>
> "In contrast, the original BFI's imbalanced content means that simple
> within-person centering removes meaningful personality information along
> with acquiescence."

Soto, C. J., John, O. P., Gosling, S. D., & Potter, J. (2008). The
developmental psychometrics of Big Five self-reports: Acquiescence, factor
structure, coherence, and differentiation from ages 10 to 20. *Journal of
Personality and Social Psychology*, 94(4), 718-737.
https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.94.4.718

Route: the Colby lab copy 403s live; retrieved via the Wayback Machine,
`https://web.archive.org/web/2019/http://www.colby.edu/psychology/labs/personality/publications/Soto_et_al_2008.pdf`.

> "At the scale level, if a scale does not have balanced keying (i.e., an
> equal number of true-keyed and false-keyed items), its internal
> consistency will be artificially inflated by the positively biased
> interitem correlations, but its validity will be decreased because
> measurement of the desired construct will be confounded with
> acquiescence."

Numbers: acquiescence variance was 0.14 at age 10 against 0.07 at age 20;
raw-data PCA produced a sixth component on which "38 of the 44 BFI items
(86%) had positive loadings"; ipsatising removed it and raised congruence
with the age-20 solution from .88 to .97 (E), .83 to .94 (C) and .84 to .95
(N).

Rammstedt, B., Goldberg, L. R., & Borg, I. (2010). The measurement
equivalence of Big Five factor markers for persons with different levels of
education. *Journal of Research in Personality*, 44(1), 53-61.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrp.2009.10.005

Route: full text open at PubMed Central, PMC2854544. This paper carries the
single most load-bearing sentence for the question as asked:

> "An even more important limitation of the present research is that it is
> based on a measure that is completely balanced in its content keying
> across each of the five dimensions. The use of fully ipsatized or deviation
> scores will only be effective to the extent to which the item pool is so
> balanced."

Raw congruence with an idealised Big Five pattern was .70 and .61 in two
data sets; after ipsatising, "the congruence coefficients were now all above
.90".

**The point.** Every one of these justifies 50/50 by the ipsatisation it
enables. Cèrcol's rater already produces a rank, which is a within-block
ipsative datum by construction. There is no person mean left to remove.
Importing the Likert-scale rule into a rank format is a category error.

**5c. What the ratio does and does not control in Cèrcol's format.**

The forced-choice design guideline is stated over *pairwise comparisons
within a block*, not over items. Same-direction comparisons yield trait
**differences**; opposite-direction comparisons yield trait **sums**; both
are needed to locate anyone in absolute terms (Brown and Maydeu-Olivares,
2011, quoted in 1c). Brown and Maydeu-Olivares's own empirical Big Five
instrument used an item ratio of **8 positive to 4 negative per trait**, 67
percent positive, and still achieved 50 percent opposite-direction
comparisons, "combined in a way that equal number of pairwise comparisons
occur between items keyed in the same direction and items keyed in opposite
directions", because the negative items sat *inside* mixed blocks.

Applied to Cèrcol: the 75/25 *block* ratio moves the pole of the block, not
the keying mix within it. Every block, positive or negative, has the same
4:6 opposite-to-same comparison structure, because the N adjective is always
the odd one out. **So the 75/25 dial does not change the quantity the
literature cares about.** Setting it to 50/50, or 90/10, or 0/100, would
leave the opposite-keyed comparison count at 4 in 10 in every case. The
ratio is inert with respect to normative recovery.

That is a different verdict from "75/25 is indefensible". It is defensible,
in the weak sense that nothing in the literature says it is wrong and the
thing it appears to be addressing is not a problem this format has. What it
is not is *load-bearing*. The documentation should stop describing it as a
"key forced-choice design constraint" (the phrase in the `buildRounds`
docstring) and describe it for what it actually does: control which end of
each factor the rater is asked to discriminate on, and expose the low-pole
vocabulary at all.

**5d. What the 75/25 ratio does control, and where it is arguably wrong.**

Two real effects, neither of them acquiescence.

*Corpus coverage.* At 13 rounds the ten positive blocks exhaust the ten
positive markers per factor, while the three negative blocks sample 3 of 10.
Every session shows all 50 positive markers and only 15 of the 50 negative
ones, drawn at random. Half the corpus is under-sampled by a factor of more
than three, which means the negative-pole half of the instrument has both
higher item-sampling variance and less content coverage. If the low-pole
markers are worth having in the corpus at all, this is the argument for
moving toward 50/50, and it is a coverage argument rather than a keying one.

*Within-block desirability and extremity matching, which is the constraint
that actually binds.* Cao and Drasgow, p. 1350:

> "Without matching the extremity of the two statements in pairwise
> preference FC scales where both statements are in the same direction
> (e.g., positive), respondents can simply choose the statement with higher
> extremity on the trait level, which will generally produce higher scores
> (Stark et al., 2012)."

With effect sizes from Table 3: desirability-matched d = .02 [−.02, .07]
against a significant moderator effect for unmatched, t(55) = −3.22;
extremity-matched d = .01 [−.06, .09] against unmatched d = .14 [.04, .24].

This maps onto Cèrcol exactly. A positive block of *outgoing / caring /
organized / calm / creative* is only a personality measurement if those five
words are equally flattering and equally strong. `buildRounds` does **no**
desirability or extremity matching: it draws one adjective per factor from a
shuffled pool of that factor's ten same-pole markers. Whatever balance
exists is a by-product of the corpus audit recorded in SCIENCE.md, not a
property of block construction. This is the highest-value change available
in this whole review, and it is a change to `buildRounds`, not to the ratio.

**5e. Reverse-keyed items carry their own costs, so more negative blocks is
not free.**

Suárez-Álvarez, J., Pedrosa, I., Lozano, L. M., García-Cueto, E., Cuesta, M.,
& Muñiz, J. (2018). Using reversed items in Likert scales: A questionable
practice. *Psicothema*, 30(2), 149-158.
https://doi.org/10.7334/psicothema2018.33 (route: open PDF at
`https://www.psicothema.com/pdf/4463.pdf`). Repeated-measures, N = 374, three
forms of the same 20-item test: all positive α = .932, all reversed α = .921,
mixed α = .879. The mixed form is the worst of the three.

İlhan, M., Güler, N., Taşdelen Teker, G., & Ergenekon, Ö. (2024). The effects
of reverse items on psychometric properties and respondents' scale scores
according to different item reversal strategies. *International Journal of
Assessment Tools in Education*, 11(1), 20-38.
https://doi.org/10.21449/ijate.1345549 (route: open PDF at
`https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1440235.pdf`). McDonald's ω: .861
all-positive, .855 antonym-reversed, .816 negation-reversed. Both mixed
forms failed a single-factor CFA and required a two-factor solution splitting
"exactly along keying direction", which is a keying method factor made
visible.

Swain, S. D., Weathers, D., & Niedrich, R. W. (2008). Assessing three sources
of misresponse to reversed Likert items. *Journal of Marketing Research*,
45(1), 116-131. https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.45.1.116 (route: abstract only,
via OpenAlex; full text paywalled at SAGE). "misresponse to reversed Likert
items averaged approximately 20%, twice the level identified as problematic
in previous simulation studies."

And directly on negation inside forced-choice blocks, Brown and
Maydeu-Olivares (2011):

> "One last comment on using negatively keyed items in forced-choice
> questionnaires concerns the use of negation. In our experience, responding
> to forced-choice blocks involving items with negation can be confusing for
> respondents; therefore straight negation should be avoided and replaced
> wherever possible with appropriate synonyms."

**Cèrcol satisfies this already.** Its low-pole markers are adjectival
(reserved, blunt, careless, anxious, conventional) rather than negation-based
(un-outgoing, not careful). That is a real design virtue and it is nowhere
written down. Contrast Goldberg's own 100, where 28 percent of terms are
negations and 20 begin with `un-`; Saucier deliberately cut that to 15
percent and four `un-` terms in the Mini-Markers.

**5f. Verdict on the brief's question.**

The 75/25 split should **not** be changed to 50/50 on acquiescence grounds,
because a forced-choice rank format does not have an acquiescence problem
and every 50/50 recommendation in the literature is scoped to rating scales
and justified by ipsatisation the format already performs.

There is a coverage argument for moving toward 50/50 (5d), and it is
modest.

The changes the literature actually supports, in priority order:

1. **Match adjectives within each block on social desirability and
   extremity.** This is the binding constraint for a same-pole block and
   `buildRounds` does not do it. Cao and Drasgow give the effect sizes.
2. **Increase the proportion of opposite-keyed comparisons per block above
   the current 4 in 10**, by building blocks that mix high-pole and low-pole
   markers rather than by moving the block ratio. This is the change that
   converts the instrument from mostly-difference information to
   difference-plus-sum information, and it is worth about .07 of recovery
   correlation in the published five-trait simulations (.863 to .929 in
   Brown and Maydeu-Olivares Simulation 2). It also requires (1), because
   mixing poles within a block makes desirability matching harder, not
   easier: this is the tension Bürkner et al. identify when they write that
   "items within a block cannot be matched for desirability ... and at the
   same time unequally keyed".
3. **Do not add negation-based low-pole markers.** The current adjectival
   ones are correct.
4. **Keep the ratio dial, but document it honestly.** It controls corpus
   coverage and which pole the rater discriminates on. It does not control
   acquiescence, and the `buildRounds` docstring calling it "a key
   forced-choice design constraint" overstates it.

## Bonus

### B1. How ipsative the shipped scoring actually is

ADR 0019 says "The scores are not strictly ipsative ... the five domain
scores sum to anything from 11 to 19 rather than to a constant." That is
correct, and the mechanism is the one identified in 1c: within a block, four
of the five adjectives share a valence and the fifth (the N marker) does not.
Every block contributes
`w_best·v_best + w_second·v_second + w_worst·v_worst`, which is a constant
only if all five valences match. They never do.

Measured against the shipped code, 20,000 uniformly random Witnesses through
`buildRounds(13)` and `computeWitnessScores`: the sum of the five domain
scores has **mean 15.22, SD 0.74**, 90 percent of sessions between 14.00 and
16.46, observed extremes 12.31 and 18.31. If the five domains were
independent at the documented per-domain SD of about 1.03, the sum would
have an SD near 2.3. The observed 0.74 means roughly 90 percent of the
between-person variance in overall level has been removed by the format.

Two things follow:

1. The ADR's "carries some level information as well as shape" is true but
   should be quantified. 0.74 against a normative 2.3 is a small amount of
   level information, and it rides entirely on the Depth channel, which
   sections 3 and 4 say a colleague judges worst.
2. Any comparison of one person's Witness profile against another's is a
   comparison of shapes, not levels. SCIENCE.md's Full Moon methodology
   already reaches this conclusion by a different route ("Comparisons are
   expressed structurally, as relative rankings of archetypes") and this is
   the psychometric reason it was the right call.

### B2. The blog describes an instrument Cèrcol does not ship

`forced-choice-personality-assessment-more-honest-data` and
`what-the-cercol-witness-instrument-measures` both describe the Witness as a
forced-choice **adjective-pair** instrument: "it presents the assessor with
pairs of personality-relevant adjectives and asks them to choose which one
better describes the person", with a worked example of "bold vs.
considerate". The shipped instrument presents five adjectives per block and
asks for three ranked picks. It has never presented pairs.

The same articles claim within-block social desirability matching ("adjective
pairs are selected to maximise discriminative information ... while keeping
social desirability values as equal as possible within each pair").
`buildRounds` does no desirability matching at all (see 5d).

They also claim "A typical Witness session takes 8-12 minutes" and
"confidence intervals that widen as N decreases". Neither is derivable from
anything in this repository: no real session has been timed, and no
confidence interval is computed anywhere in `witness-scoring.js` or the Full
Moon report.

### B3. Corpus provenance does not match what the code header claims

`src/data/witness-adjectives.js` opens with "Derived from public-domain IPIP
lexical markers (Goldberg; ipip.ori.org)". Two problems.

First, `ipip.ori.org` does not publish adjective markers. The IPIP Big-Five
Factor Markers hosted there (fetched directly) are *statements*: "Am the life
of the party", "Get stressed out easily", "Have a vivid imagination". The
adjective tradition is Goldberg (1992) in *Psychological Assessment* and
Saucier (1994) in *Journal of Personality Assessment*.

Second, the overlap with the best-known published adjective marker set is
small. Saucier's 40 Mini-Markers were extracted verbatim from the author's
instrument sheet and intersected with Cèrcol's 100:

| Pole | Overlap with the 40 Mini-Markers |
|---|---|
| E+ | 3: talkative, bold, energetic |
| E− | 3: quiet, withdrawn, shy |
| A+ | 3: warm, cooperative, kind |
| A− | 2: harsh, cold |
| C+ | 1: organized |
| C− | 2: careless, disorganized |
| N+ | 1: moody |
| N− | 0 |
| O+ | 2: creative, imaginative |
| O− | 0 |
| **Total** | **17 of 100** |

Eighty-three of the hundred are wordings that do not appear in the Saucier
set, and the two poles with zero overlap are exactly the two the literature
says are hardest (N− per 2b, O− per Soto and John's note that "many more
adjectives in natural language describe high levels than low levels of
intellectual and creative interests"). "Modelled on the Goldberg and Saucier
adjective-marker tradition; item wordings are Cèrcol's own" is defensible.
"Derived from IPIP lexical markers" is not.

Saucier's keying balance is worth recording alongside: the 40 Mini-Markers
are keyed 4:4 on Extraversion, Agreeableness and Conscientiousness, but 2:6
on Emotional Stability and 6:2 on Intellect. Twenty positive and twenty
negative overall, not balanced within every factor. Cèrcol's 10:10 on every
factor is stricter than the published instrument, which is a strength
everywhere except N (2b).

### B4. An item-level inconsistency between SCIENCE.md and the shipped data

SCIENCE.md records a corpus audit that removed low-pole adjectives reading as
socially positive, naming "*spontaneous*, *flexible*, *carefree* on C−" and
"*practical*, *realistic*, *pragmatic* on O−" as having been "replaced by
less ambiguous markers". `flexible` is still in C− in the shipped data,
alongside `casual` and `informal`, and `straightforward` is still in O−.

On the John and Robins evidence this matters in a specific way. Their
desirability effect is *curvilinear*: highly desirable and highly undesirable
traits both elicit lower agreement than neutral ones. So the fix is not to
make low-pole markers more clearly undesirable; it is to make the five
adjectives within a block similar in desirability. A C− block pairing
`careless` and `unreliable` with `flexible` and `casual` is unmatched in
exactly the way Cao and Drasgow warn about (5d).

### B5. Two more citation defects

- `forced-choice-personality-assessment-more-honest-data` cites Stark,
  Chernyshenko and Drasgow (2005) as *Applied Psychological Measurement*,
  29(3), 184-201. The correct pagination is **184-203**, and the article has
  a DOI the blog omits:
  Stark, S., Chernyshenko, O. S., & Drasgow, F. (2005). An IRT approach to
  constructing and scoring pairwise preference items involving stimuli on
  different dimensions: The multi-unidimensional pairwise-preference model.
  *Applied Psychological Measurement*, 29(3), 184-203.
  https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621604273988
- The same article's green callout asserts "Research shows forced-choice
  reduces social desirability bias by 40-60% compared to Likert scales" with
  no citation attached. Replace with the Cao and Drasgow figures in 1d.

### B6. The `witness-scoring.js` docstring is stale

The file header still describes a two-pick instrument ("The witness picks one
as BEST fit and one as WORST fit", "BEST pick → factor_votes +=
adjective.valence, WORST pick → factor_votes -= adjective.valence"), the
`buildRounds` docstring still says "@param {number} totalRounds - number of
rounds (default 20)" and documents the 20-round polarity sequence, and
`computeInterimRole`'s comment says "an SD near 0.50" where ADR 0019 records
0.93 and then 1.03. `computeInterimRole` also filters on
`r.best !== null && r.worst !== null`, ignoring `second`, which is
inconsistent with the three-pick design being mandatory.
